# Price low, teach early, ship Early Access

Last Lightkeepers is not an outlier genre bet — it is structurally the same game as the two biggest co-op hits of the last three years, and both of them cost **$9.99 in Early Access**. Lethal Company and R.E.P.O. are PvE co-op extraction loops (deploy, loot against a quota, extract, lose everything on a wipe) that were marketed as "co-op horror," and they sit at 487,160 and 185,642 Steam reviews at 97% and 96% positive respectively ([SteamSpy](https://steamspy.com/api.php)). Every game in the dataset that attached the word "extraction" to a $19.99–$29.99 price sits at 54–75% positive with populations in the hundreds. That is the single most decision-changing finding in this research, and it collides directly with the project's one acknowledged pricing unknown. Three other collisions matter as much. **Steam's indie on-ramp changed in June 2026** — Popular Upcoming's threshold rose from roughly 7,000 wishlists to an estimated 100,000, Next Fest's follower gain for the *top decile* of entrants fell to **+63**, and the only Valve-side levers left are a release date locked 60 days out and real wishlists ([howtomarketagame](https://howtomarketagame.com/2026/06/25/how-the-steam-personal-calendar-affects-your-launch/); [GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/who-won-october-2026s-steam-next)). **UE5 costs $0 in engine fees below roughly 66,700 units but silently sets a minimum spec that excludes Steam Deck**, because one moving shadow-casting light per player is a Virtual Shadow Map problem and the engine's answer to it requires ray-tracing-capable hardware ([Epic: MegaLights](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/megalights-in-unreal-engine?lang=en-US)). And **the "no separate tutorial" decision is the one the research contradicts outright**: in four-player co-op the player who lights the lantern and the player who meets the monster are usually different people, so neither experiences the rule — and every systemically complex game in this cohort, Hunt, Tarkov, Phasmophobia, Deep Rock, Darktide, retrofitted onboarding later under player pressure.

## Your two biggest competitors are your game with a different label, and they cost $9.99

The reframing first, because it changes what "comparable" means. Lethal Company and R.E.P.O. are not adjacent to a PvE co-op extraction game; they *are* one. Both ask a small friend group to enter a space, gather valuables against a rising demand, and get out, with a wipe paying nothing. Neither is marketed with the word "extraction," and that labelling difference tracks almost perfectly with commercial outcome. On one side of the line: Lethal Company at **$9.99** (487,160 reviews, 97.2% positive, 239,369 all-time peak CCU, solo developer), R.E.P.O. at **$9.99** (185,642 reviews, 96.5%, 266,908 peak), Content Warning at **$7.99** (145,091 reviews, 94.8%), DEVOUR at **$9.99** (82,514 reviews, 91%). On the other: Level Zero: Extraction at $19.99 (**2,825 reviews at 54.2% positive**, one concurrent player, now permanently discounted to $4.99), Incursion Red River at $21.99 (5,217 reviews, 74.8%), The Forever Winter at $29.99 (19,046 reviews, 72.3%), Marauders at $29.99 (20,679 reviews, 71.4%, now six concurrent players). Prices, review counts and peaks were read from Steam's own storefront API and SteamSpy's mirror of Steam's review counters on 2026-10-27; the owner bands used below are SteamSpy *model* output and should be treated as order-of-magnitude only ([SteamSpy](https://steamspy.com/api.php); [SteamCharts](https://steamcharts.com/app/1966720)).

Generic Steam pricing data points the opposite way, and that conflict must not be smoothed over. AppMagic's analysis of the top 1,000 Steam games reports $19.99 as the most common tier (147 games) and argues it delivers more gross revenue per sale than $14.99 ([AppMagic](https://appmagic.rocks/research/steam-pricing-research) — note the researcher could not render that page's data and took the figures from a search-index summary, so verify before quoting them as precise). The resolution the clustering suggests, and it is an inference nobody sources, is that co-op horror is bought by friend groups of three or four at once: the real transaction at $9.99 is $30–$40, and a $19.99 sticker turns a four-pack into an $80 decision. Phasmophobia is the apparent counterexample at $19.99 and 20 million copies — a publisher-stated figure, the only firm long-run number in the genre ([Kinetic Games](https://x.com/KineticGamesDev/status/1852024963378635238)) — but it launched into Early Access in September 2020 and has accrued five years of content and console ports. It is not a pricing precedent available to a 2027 launch.

**On the price row, the research is unambiguous and it is permissive, not restrictive.** Nothing in this dataset supports $19.99 for a first solo co-op extraction game without a publisher's marketing spend. The honest range is $9.99 to $14.99, and the case for the upper end is net revenue per copy, not evidence: third-party models converge on roughly **$6.00–$7.50 net on a $14.99 list price** after VAT, regional pricing and refunds, against about $6.99 net on $9.99 before those deductions ([beancount.io](https://beancount.io/blog/2026/07/09/steam-revenue-share-indie-game-developer-bookkeeping-guide); [creex.team](https://creex.team/publishing/en/calc/)). Every one of those net figures is modelled, not measured; Valve publishes no regional-pricing haircut, and the four estimates the researcher found (15–30%, 20–25%, 40–50%, and a 0.51–0.88 blended factor) cannot be reconciled.

On **what to sell after the price**, the research supplies a warning rather than an answer. No source in this set addresses post-purchase monetisation for a paid co-op horror game, and the researcher found no wishlist-to-purchase conversion data by price tier for any genre — a genuine hole, searched for repeatedly. What exists is the failure mode: Content Warning's documented onboarding complaint is not about its verbs but its *shop*, with players specifically lacking information about item values ([Gameshorizon](https://gameshorizon.com/review/content-warning-review-brilliant-multiplayer-experience/)). Whatever gets added after the price has to teach its own value, and the evidence says discovery teaches verbs well and economies badly.

Two more structural facts about this genre's revenue shape deserve to sit next to the price decision. First, **the launch month essentially is the revenue**: GameSensor estimates Demonologist's first Early Access month at over 90% of the studio's total Steam sales ([GameSensor](https://gamesensor.info/news/demonologist_early_access)) — an estimate, from a modelling service, but the most actionable single number in the set. Second, **the modal good outcome is not Lethal Company**. A well-reviewed, correctly-priced, correctly-scoped co-op horror game that does not catch a creator wave lands near Murky Divers or FOREWARNED: 2,000–7,000 reviews, 1,300–2,100 peak CCU, a few hundred thousand units. Under the 26–50 sales-per-review range this dataset supports, that is roughly $1M–$3M gross — an inference built from hard review counts plus a heuristic, not a sourced figure for either game. Plan for that band; the top of the distribution is not plannable. Semiwork's own stated doctrine is the closest thing to advice from inside the genre: after Voidigo took six years and sold poorly, the studio decided to "just fail quickly this time," and R.E.P.O. — their first Unity project, learned as they went — followed ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/horror/lets-just-fail-quickly-this-time-semiwork-took-a-big-risk-on-repo-after-its-first-game-took-6-years-to-make-and-didnt-sell-very-well/)).

Finally, the **private-lobby decision is strongly supported, and for a mechanical reason**. Level Zero's players blame its collapse on competing with free extraction shooters for strangers' time — "with delta force coming out another FREE mp extraction game you just cant really survive on being a paid one" ([Steam forum](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1456940/discussions/0/4840897430886070908/)) — and Yager shut down The Cycle: Frontier after a cheater surge caused a "significant decrease in our player base," calling it not financially viable ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/yager-sunsetting-the-cycle-frontier-after-cheaters-cause-irreparable-damage)). Both failure modes require stranger matchmaking. A game where four friends bring their own lobby cannot have an empty queue and has almost no cheating incentive. The one cost of the decision is reach: R.E.P.O. went to **six** players, which exactly fits a streamer collaboration roster, and a four-cap is at the tighter end of what turns one creator's interest into a multi-channel event.

## Steam's indie on-ramp closed in June 2026, and Playtest is now the only free de-risking

The discovery landscape a 2027 launch faces is not the one the folklore describes. Valve raised the Popular Upcoming threshold from roughly 7,000 wishlists to an estimated 100,000 around June 2026 — Valve itself only said the tab was "updated in response to player feedback in order to better capture the most anticipated releases"; the 100,000 figure is Chris Zukowski's estimate, not a Valve number ([howtomarketagame](https://howtomarketagame.com/2026/06/25/how-the-steam-personal-calendar-affects-your-launch/); [PC Guide](https://www.pcguide.com/news/steams-new-100000-wishlist-rule-means-many-indie-devs-will-have-to-rely-on-a-different-feature-to-be-discovered/)). Popular Upcoming should be deleted from the plan. Its replacement in function is the **Personal Calendar**, a per-user widget drawn from each player's wishlist, follow list and play history, running from roughly 60 days before release to 30 days after; Valve's relayed guidance is to set the release date — "EA or 1.0!" — at least **60 days before launch**, and that the more real wishlists you have, the likelier placement is ([GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/who-won-october-2026s-steam-next); [Inven Global](https://www.invenglobal.com/articles/23483/changes-to-the-steam-shop-algorithm-the-rise-of-the-personal-calendar)). Because the Calendar window extends a month *past* launch, a late date slip now costs placement during the exact window it was earning.

Next Fest has stopped being a discovery mechanism. October 2026 drew about **7,300 demos, up 66.6% from June's ~4,400**, while top-10 median wishlist gain fell 44% and — the number that matters for a small game — **follower gain for the top 10% of entrants fell to +63, down 48% on June 2026 and 64% on October 2025**, which GameDiscoverCo describes as "fairly representative of all tiers" ([GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/who-won-october-2026s-steam-next)). If the best decile gained 63 followers, the median entrant gained approximately nothing. Each game gets exactly one Next Fest, ever, and cannot release until the event ends ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/upcoming_events/nextfest/2026october)). Spend it at peak off-platform momentum, not as a rescue. The free alternatives are genre Fests, which do not consume that allowance: Valve lists **Couch Co-Op Fest for 8–15 February 2027** and **Steam Scream V Fest for 26 October – 2 November 2026** ([Steamworks: Upcoming Events](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/upcoming_events)). The next Next Fests are 22 February – 1 March 2027 and 14–21 June 2027; the February registration deadline was not found.

For planning, the defensible wishlist arithmetic is a range, not a number. Zukowski's June-2026 tiers put wishlists at launch at 5,000 (bronze), 8,000 (silver), 50,000 (gold), 90,000 (diamond), with first-week conversion of 11.93% at the $1k-revenue tier rising to 25–27% at the $100k–$1M tiers ([howtomarketagame Benchmarks](https://howtomarketagame.com/benchmarks/)). GameDiscoverCo's stricter measure gives a median of **0.15× first-week sales per wishlist above 25,000 wishlists, falling to 0.10× for games priced above $10** ([GameDiscoverCo](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/gamediscoverco-the-state-of-steam)). Ten to twenty percent of wishlists becoming launch-week sales is the honest band. Note that the widely quoted 20% conversion table is dated June 2020, and that no source gives wishlist conversion for co-op or multiplayer games, where one friend buying and pushing three others plausibly behaves differently.

The most useful mechanical finding for a project whose loop has never been playtested is the **risk ladder**, and it runs Playtest → demo → Early Access → 1.0. Steam Playtest is a child appID with no store page of its own, and critically, **Playtest activity does not affect wishlists, reviews, refunds, achievements or trading cards for the main game** ([Steamworks: Playtest](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/playtest)). A janky Playtest build costs nothing permanent. Its experimental Friend Invites feature lets a tester bring one to three friends who have been on their list for at least 30 days — which for a 1–4 co-op game is the only honest way to test the loop, because the real unit of play is a group. The country filter lets testers be concentrated into overlapping time zones. Playtest's weakness is that it builds no public signal at all: it substitutes for Early Access's *testing* function, not its marketing function.

Early Access is where the evidence turns uncomfortable and confirms what the genre already shows. GameDiscoverCo's 2026 analysis of Steam Early Access graduates found a median **1 year 3 months** in EA and that **only 21% made more revenue at 1.0 than at their EA launch** ([GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/what-happened-to-early-access-graduates)). EA does not fail; it *relocates the commercial peak to EA day one*, which for a solo developer is arguably good news — the real launch happens while the game is still changeable — but only if EA day one gets full launch discipline. The measurable price is refunds: GameDiscoverCo's survey of nearly 150 developers gives a median refund rate of **12.4% for Early Access versus 8% for 1.0 launches** ([GameDiscoverCo](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/gamediscoverco-steam-refunds-research)). Every top co-op horror outcome except Content Warning launched in Early Access. Four points of refund rate is cheap for a real launch at a changeable moment.

Demos carry a specific, underappreciated hazard for this game. Since the July 2024 demo update a demo is always a separate App ID, and a separate store page is optional — but enabling it **lets demo players leave user reviews**, with no documented mechanism for clearing them, on a page that links to the paid game ([Steamworks: Demos](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/application/demos)). Valve is explicit that demos get no visibility advantage: they "can appear anywhere in Steam that a free game could appear." Wishlist release notifications for the demo **may only be triggered once** in its first 14 days. For a co-op game where a short demo session may mean "couldn't get friends online" rather than "didn't like it," permanent public reviews on that build are a bad trade; the default demo-as-button configuration is the lower-risk choice. The researcher found **no direct evidence whatsoever** on whether a demo helps or hurts a horror co-op game, and no controlled capsule A/B data exists anywhere — the widely repeated claim that simpler capsules win is unsourced assertion from a vendor blog. The one mechanically solid 2026 fact is that the store redesign added hover micro-trailers, which makes the first three seconds of the trailer a second capsule.

Two things to drop from the plan. **Steam Deck Verified is the worst cost/benefit item in this research**: the claimed 20–40% sales uplift is an unsourced vendor-blog figure with no dataset behind it, the device is listed at **$789** as of October 2026 with third-party estimates putting weekly unit sales down ~80% after the May 2026 price increase (a figure whose critics note rests on two layers of estimation), and UE5's baseline GPU cost makes the criteria genuinely expensive to hit ([boilingsteam](https://boilingsteam.com/the-great-steam-deck-volume-crash/); [Xenospectrum](https://xenospectrum.com/en/steam-deck-price-sales-estimate/); [gtstu](https://gtstu.com/steam-deck-optimization-indie/)). The lighting plan below excludes the Deck anyway. And **the "first 10 reviews unlock visibility" rule is not policy** — Zukowski originated it in 2022, then reported in January 2025 that Valve representatives told him directly there is no such rule, that Discovery Queue access does not depend on it, and that the only change at 10 reviews is the rating icon appearing ([howtomarketagame](https://howtomarketagame.com/2025/01/20/steam-vs-people-algorithms/)). Reviews matter enormously as social proof and conversion; the gate does not exist. On launch *timing*, the researcher found essentially no reliable month-or-day data; the 60-day date lock is now the whole timing rule.

## UE5 is free until 66,700 units and bills you in shadow milliseconds instead

The licensing answer is clean and the decision is safe on cost. Epic charges **5% of gross revenue above the first $1,000,000 lifetime gross per product**, and nothing below it; at a $15 gross price that threshold is roughly **66,700 units**, above which Epic takes about $0.75/unit, or ~7% of net once Steam's 30% is accounted for. The 2024 seat-based subscription (~$1,850/seat/year) explicitly does **not** cover games ([Epic pricing announcement](https://www.unrealengine.com/en-US/blog/we-are-updating-unreal-engine-twinmotion-and-realitycapture-pricing-in-late-april); [Meta Horizon docs](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/unreal/unreal-oculus-license/)). Two caveats are load-bearing. Epic's EULA, licence and change-log pages all return **HTTP 403 to automated fetching**, so the 5% rate above is quoted from Meta's documentation rather than Epic's own words, and the $1M exclusion language is quoted from Epic's change log — **open the EULA in a browser before any financial planning**. Separately, EULA update 21 added a restriction on using MetaHuman characters and animation curves with AI or similar technologies ([EULA Change Log](https://www.unrealengine.com/eula-change-log/unreal)); for an AI-assisted workflow that clause should be read verbatim. Royalty administration is a small calendar obligation — quarterly reporting due 45 days after each quarter close, plus a Release Form — and sources conflict on whether that form is a condition of the exclusion or merely a report. Licensing is not a differentiator at this scale: Godot is free outright, Unity Personal is free below a $200,000 trailing-twelve-month cap (hit at roughly 13,300 units, five times sooner than Epic's threshold), and all three cost $0 at the realistic outcome band.

The lighting finding is the one that changes the store page. **Lumen's global illumination is close to the wrong tool for this game, and the real cost is shadows.** Epic's published Lumen budget is 4 ms for GI plus reflections at 60 fps, 1080p internal, on current-gen consoles — roughly a quarter of a 16.7 ms frame — and Lumen Scene Lighting "picks a small set of the most important lights per surface cache tile, so its cost depends less on total light count" ([Epic: Lumen Performance Guide](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/lumen-performance-guide-for-unreal-engine)). A nearly-black interior has almost nothing to bounce, so Lumen is *cheaper* here than in a typical scene. The expensive part is the opposite: Epic's own documentation states that **"a light that moves invalidates every page for that light,"** destroying the Virtual Shadow Map caching benefit entirely, and gives the scale as "three shadow-casting Movable Point lights could take upwards of **14 milliseconds** to render shadow depths, while with cached shadows enabled those same lights may take around **1 millisecond**" ([Epic: Movable Light Mobility](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/movable-light-mobility-in-unreal-engine)). That is a single-scene figure for three point lights with and without caching, not a benchmark for one moving spotlight — but the direction is unambiguous, and in a four-player game there are **four independently moving shadow-casting lights, i.e. four times the invalidation problem**. Expect the shadow line in the GPU profiler to dominate, not the GI line.

Epic's answer to exactly this case is **MegaLights**, a new direct lighting path using stochastic importance sampling with a fixed number of rays per pixel, so cost is "mostly constant" rather than per-light; it replaces Virtual Shadow Map projection, and Epic's guidance is that "because cost stays mostly constant, all lights can cast shadows" ([Epic: MegaLights](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/megalights-in-unreal-engine?lang=en-US)). It is also the biggest technical risk in the plan, for three reasons. It is **Beta in 5.7 and tagged experimental in the 5.8 documentation** — Epic's own labels are inconsistent across its own surfaces, which means a 5.8/5.9 upgrade may change the game's core visual identity. It requires **current-gen consoles or ray-tracing-capable PCs only**: no mobile, no Switch, no PS4 or Xbox One, and it is incompatible with the Forward Renderer. And Epic's docs never address the low-light-count end, so **whether a one-light dark interior pays the same fixed cost as a fifty-light scene is genuinely unknown** — if it does, plain deferred lighting plus VSM may be cheaper for this game despite MegaLights being the obvious conceptual fit. That should be the first thing profiled, and it is cheap to test.

**The minimum-spec consequence is a commercial decision disguised as a graphics one, and it should be made now.** Lumen's GPU floor is DirectX 12 with SM6 on NVIDIA RTX 2000-series, AMD RX 6000-series or Intel Arc A-series or newer ([Box.co.uk](https://box.co.uk/blog/unreal-engine-development-pc-specs)); MegaLights requires ray-tracing-capable PCs; and Lumen's software detail-trace path was **deprecated in UE 5.6**, with Lumen "moving toward a single hardware ray tracing path at 60 Hz" ([Tom Looman](https://tomlooman.com/unreal-engine-5-7-performance-highlights/)). Those are the same line. Designing around Software Lumen in 2026 is designing against a deprecated path, which raises the effective floor over the project's life. The store page therefore excludes the Steam Deck and a meaningful share of the Steam hardware survey's low end — which is consistent with dropping Deck Verified, but it is a reach cost that should be chosen rather than discovered. **No reliable 1080p or 1440p 60-fps Lumen benchmark on mid-range PC hardware exists in any primary source**; Epic publishes console millisecond budgets only, and the third-party tables that give PC numbers are unsourced. This question can only be settled by profiling on the target machine.

Three cost cuts fall straight out of Epic's own guidance and are free for this art direction. Replacing Lumen Reflections with screen-space reflections saved **~1 ms on Xbox Series S**, and a dark interior hides the difference — Still Wakes the Deep, the one well-documented UE5 horror comparable, **deliberately cut mirror reflections** on cost and artistic grounds and reviewers did not punish it ([Epic Lumen Performance Guide](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/lumen-performance-guide-for-unreal-engine); [Neowin](https://www.neowin.net/reviews/review-still-wakes-the-deep-pc---a-well-optimised-unreal-engine-5-horror-game/)). Clamping the reflection roughness threshold down from its 0.4 default in a Post Process Volume costs nothing where wet floors are the only payoff. And keeping the lantern's attenuation radius tight is both cheaper — Epic notes large-radius dynamic lights cost more — and better horror. Still Wakes the Deep is also the cautionary precedent: it runs **Software Lumen for GI and reflections plus Virtual Shadow Maps on UE 5.3.2**, chosen explicitly for *production velocity* rather than image quality ("Light maps was just a huge bottleneck for us... it prevented us from iterating quickly"), and shipped with visible Lumen noise and occlusion artifacts in dark scenes ([Creative Bloq](https://www.creativebloq.com/3d/video-game-design/how-unreal-engine-5-3-made-still-wakes-the-deep-more-terrifyingly-beautiful); [DSOGaming](https://www.dsogaming.com/pc-performance-analyses/still-wakes-the-deep-benchmarks-pc-performance-analysis/)). A darker game with a single moving light will show those artifacts *more*, because there is no ambient light to mask temporal noise. Epic separately warns that low World Space Radiance Cache probe-update budgets cause **lighting pops during fast camera motion** — which a flashlight game is, continuously. Noise and popping tuning belongs on the schedule as a named task, not as polish. The lightmap argument, incidentally, is *stronger* for one person than for The Chinese Room: a bake queue is a serialisation point a solo developer cannot parallelise around, and AI agents cannot shorten a lightmap bake.

On the rest of the engine's bill, the picture is mixed and mostly manageable. Nanite has a roughly fixed per-object overhead that does not shrink as meshes simplify, so a room full of 200–5,000-triangle props is its worst case and selective enablement is the answer ([Strayspark](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/nanite-performance-cost-when-to-use)) — but there is a genuine tension nobody resolves, because community VSM testing found many local lights perform *far better* with Nanite geometry than non-Nanite (a UE 5.0/5.1-era thread, possibly stale) ([Epic forums](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/virtual-shadow-and-local-lights-performance/677724)). The plausible split is to Nanite what actually occludes the beam and leave the small decorative items traditional, and that needs a profiled A/B in the real level rather than a rule. Glass, dirty panes, hanging plastic and particle cards — standard dark-interior vocabulary — are all on the don't-Nanite list. Shader stutter has a real default-on mitigation in PSO precaching, but it requires work the developer must do: hold the loading screen until `FShaderPipelineCache::NumPrecompilesRemaining()` reaches zero, or "visible popping and hitches can occur," and measure with `-clearPSODriverCache` or the driver cache hides the hitches ([Epic: PSO Precaching](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/pso-precaching-for-unreal-engine)). Traversal/streaming stutter is a *different problem* that survives a warm shader cache and has no engine-level fix in anything through 5.7 ([ProudlyGamer](https://proudlygamer.com/post/why-unreal-engine-5-games-stutter/)). On hardware, Epic's 12–16-core recommendation for local C++ builds and PSO precaching's up-to-2 GB per compile thread interact badly and no source mentions it: 16 cores with 32 GB RAM is a configuration that will thrash, and 64 GB plus 2 TB is the defensible solo spec — an inference from combining two sources, not a sourced recommendation. Texture-size caps are an unusually cheap win for this art direction specifically, since most surfaces are seen at low light levels in a moving cone (one developer reported 2 GB down to under 1 GB from Device Profile caps), and a plugin audit typically saves 50–200 MB for a checkbox exercise. **No Steam-specific packaging guidance was found at all** — nothing on depot structure, SteamPipe practice, or whether UE5's compressed pak layout defeats Steam's delta patching, which matters for a live co-op game shipping frequent updates. That is a real and plausible concern with no source addressing it.

Two findings here deserve to be recorded as awkward rather than filed away. First, **the AI-agent workflow argues against Blueprints more strongly than the sources themselves do**. Every source frames the binary-`.uasset` diff problem as a team-size problem — "Blueprint assets are binary and don't produce readable diffs, which becomes a real team problem above two developers" ([Game-Ace](https://game-ace.com/blog/unreal-engine-blueprints/)). But an AI coding agent's entire interface is text: a Blueprint graph is effectively invisible to it, undiffable and unreviewable. A solo developer with AI agents is in the "above two developers" regime from day one, not because of headcount but because the collaborator is text-only. That inference is the researcher's, not any source's, and it is the most important claim in that section. The corollary is that the usual solo argument for Blueprints partly collapses: they exist to remove the friction of writing code, which is precisely what agents are best at, while their binary cost remains. The hybrid that follows is C++ for everything agents must own — gameplay, replication, save/load, the lantern's light-state logic, AI behaviour — with Blueprints kept for level scripting, UMG wiring, Sequencer and material graphs, and tunables in Data Assets. Second, **one experienced developer's stated reason for returning to Unity from UE5 was that AI tools made Unity projects easier to finish** ([Medium](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/ive-moved-to-unity-from-unreal-engine-because-of-ai-144112579fd9)). The engine decision is locked and this is one opinion piece with no systematic evidence either way, but the actionable form is real: expect agents to be less reliable on UE5 C++ than on Unity C#, prefer well-documented engine subsystems over clever ones, and verify agent-written UE code more aggressively. The broader solo-developer regret pattern in the sources — all forum posts and blog opinion, with severe selection bias in both directions — is not about the engine's capability but about scope: nobody says UE5 couldn't do what they wanted, they say the project got too big to steer alone. Four things in this research are genuinely not solo-doable and should be avoided by design or budgeted as contract work: diagnosing novel traversal stutter from profiler captures, graphics-level Lumen/VSM/MegaLights tuning beyond documented CVars, engine source modification, and distributed build infrastructure.

## Steam hands you the entire network stack for one recoupable $100

For a four-player friends-only PvE game the networking answer is almost embarrassingly cheap, and the whole confirmed cash cost is the **$100 per-app Steam Direct fee, recouped from a later payout once the product reaches $1,000 in adjusted gross revenue** ([Valve: Steam Direct app fee](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/appfee)). A listen server — one player hosts, the host's process is authoritative — is the standard and essentially the only budget-viable topology, and the shipped comparables confirm it: R.E.P.O. runs on Photon's relay and voice stack, and Lethal Company is community-reported (from an extracted plugin list, not a developer statement) to use Unity Netcode for GameObjects plus Dissonance ([Photon blog](https://blog.photonengine.com/r-e-p-o-multiplayer-success-powered-by-photon/); [Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1966720/discussions/0/4364627964252165209)). Note the category error waiting there: **Dissonance is Unity-only middleware and is not an option for UE5.**

The shipping path for Steam in 2026 is **OSSv1 (`OnlineSubsystemSteam`) plus the `SteamSockets` plugin**. The engine ships native OnlineServices/OSSv2 backends only for EOS and Null, Epic's docs state the OSSv2 plugins "have not been tested in shipping titles," and an Epic staff reply recommends OSSv1 for Steam with no timeline for parity ([Epic forums](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/ossv2-multiplatform-viability-and-steam-readiness/2699262)). Steam Sockets "provides encryption, DDoS protection, NAT traversal, and ping calculation for listen servers," and the decisive sentence is Epic's own: **"Listen servers always use Steam's network"** ([Epic: Using Steam Sockets](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/using-steam-sockets-in-unreal-engine)). Enabling the plugin *is* the NAT-punchthrough implementation. Valve's Steam Datagram Relay carries the traffic — "all P2P connections are automatically relayed over the backbone when appropriate," hiding player IPs and often improving ping ([Valve: Steam Networking](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/multiplayer/networking)). Valve never states in retrievable words that SDR is free; the correct phrasing is that there is **no published charge**, and no source addresses whether any fair-use cap exists. The limitation that needs a deliberate decision is that "Steam Sockets builds can only connect to other Steam Sockets builds" — there is no IP-direct fallback unless a second net driver config ships, which matters because one vendor blog reports a 2026 Steam Networking regression routing players in several regions to distant relays for roughly three months (unverified). Since OSSv1 is correct today and will not be correct for the life of the project, **the single highest-leverage architectural decision is to wrap every online call behind a thin project-owned interface** so the eventual OSSv2 migration is one file.

The invite flow is also mostly free. Steam supplies the overlay friends list, the invite UI and notification, the chat link and the process launch; what must be implemented is lobby create/destroy with a friends-only policy, the `connect` rich-presence key that makes the "Join Game" button appear, the join-requested callback for when the game is already running, and the **`+connect_lobby <lobby id>` cold-start path** for when it is not — read via `ISteamApps::GetLaunchCommandLine` rather than the OS command line ([Valve: Matchmaking & Lobbies](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/multiplayer/matchmaking); [Facepunch wiki](https://wiki.facepunch.com/steamworks/SteamApps.CommandLine)). The cold-start path is the one most often skipped and the one most needed, because the friend who does not have the game open is exactly the person you are pulling in. The glue from "joined Steam lobby" to Unreal's `JoinSession` and `ClientTravel` is where nearly every reported failure lives — Steam lobby membership and an Unreal net session are two separate things the developer must keep in sync, and **no authoritative end-to-end UE5 tutorial confirmed working on 5.5+ was found**. Enforce the four-player cap in both the Steam lobby's max-members and Unreal's session, because lobby metadata can be stale.

EOS is the tempting alternative and the research says no for this game. Epic announced Easy Anti-Cheat and in-game voice as **free through EOS**, it needs no Epic Games Store presence, and Epic's own guidelines require account linking to be player-initiated ([Epic Games](https://www.epicgames.com/site/en-US/news/epic-online-services-launches-free-in-game-voice-and-easy-anti-cheat); [Epic: Cross-Platform Guidelines](https://dev.epicgames.com/docs/epic-games-store/requirements-guidelines/distribution-requirements/multiplayer-reqs)). But for a Steam-only game EOS buys exactly one thing Steam does not already give free — voice — at the cost of a "Requires 3rd-Party Account" store-page disclosure and documented player resentment across multiple titles' forums. For a horror co-op whose growth depends on friends inviting friends, any extra click between "accept invite" and "in the game" is a conversion loss. The researcher **could not retrieve Epic's EOS pricing page** (404), so the free-tier claim rests on a 2021-era announcement plus third-party and competitor pages, and **could not confirm whether EOS voice works with Steam-only auth and no visible Epic prompt** — the pivotal question for a hybrid, unresolved. Proximity voice is the design feature most credited for Lethal Company's virality ("getting it right with incredible sound design, alongside proximity chat was a must"), and both headline comparables bought it rather than built it ([Game Design Library](https://www.gamedesignlibrary.com/post/proximity-chat-changes-the-game-how-lethal-company-s-game-design-innovated-multiplayer-horror-games)). **Steam's own voice API is the zero-cost, zero-friction candidate and is entirely uncovered by the sources retrieved** — no quality, codec or UE5 integration detail — which makes it both the likely best answer and a genuine research gap. Published per-MAU pricing for Vivox and ODIN could not be confirmed from either vendor's own page and should not be quoted; the per-MAU figure that circulates for Vivox is for Safe Text moderation, not voice. Proximity itself is the developer's work regardless of vendor: it is attaching the decoded stream to a 3D audio component with distance falloff.

Replication is an architectural constraint from day one, not a feature flag, and three of this game's specific systems are expensive in specific ways. Epic's position is that building with multiplayer in mind "will not consume much more time compared to a single-player game" ([Epic: Networking Overview](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/networking-overview-for-unreal-engine)); retrofitting is reported to overrun from 2–3 months to 6–9 (a vendor-blog generalisation, not a study), and one studio's first-hand account is blunter: single-player code "assumes one authority," and "that assumption cost us months" ([Blackscar](https://blackscarstudio.com/blog/multiplayer-lessons-goffy-clash)). **A light that affects an AI's targeting is a classic host-only bug waiting to happen**: the light's visual state replicates, but the AI's decision must be server-side, so the server needs its own cheap authoritative illumination model — a trace, volume or registered-light query — entirely separate from rendering. If the evaluation touches anything client-side, the server and clients will disagree about whether a player is lit, and *only the host will see correct behaviour*. **AI perception is the one with a hard citation: it does not replicate**, and AI controllers exist only on the server, so any client-side reaction to a monster's awareness needs a deliberately hand-authored replicated state enum ([Epic forums](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/help-with-ai-perception-multiplayer-unreal-engine/1817921)). **The Storm Charge is architecturally cheap but only if it lives in GameState from day one**, which is exactly what a single-player build never does — it puts the meter in a GameInstance or singleton subsystem. Mutations must be server-only, and because `OnRep` is change-driven and does not fire for a value equal to the class default, a meter whose threshold crossings drive client feedback needs a sequence counter rather than naive change detection ([Samuel Bouchet](https://samuel-bouchet.fr/posts/2026-07-07-unreal-coop-listen-server/)). Host migration should be treated as out of scope — Unreal has never had it, the workaround requires a master server backend — and a run-based extraction loop is the ideal shape for living without it, because a host drop costs one run rather than a campaign.

Two practices are nearly free and pay the most. **Play as the client, with 60–100 ms emulated latency, in separate processes, from week one** — host-only correctness is the dominant bug class and the host never experiences the latency bugs, so they ship undetected. And on save architecture, the clean split that sidesteps the entire genre complaint class is that **world and run state belong to the host while per-player progression belongs to the player's own local save**, written from an authoritative end-of-run summary the host replicates out. The shipped alternatives are all worse and all generate the same complaints: Evil West's guest "gets everything that the host gets" but none of it transfers to their own file; Atlas Fallen simply does not save joiner progress; Dying Light clamps guest progress back to the host's, which is both the worst player experience in the citations and more complex than either alternative ([Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1230530/discussions/0/6363075944119256933/?ctp=2); [Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/app/239140/discussions/0/1620599015878291699/)). An extraction loop maps onto the clean split unusually well, because the run is ephemeral and host-owned while meta-progression is durable and locally owned. On cheating: the engine enforces server authority whether you want it or not — client writes to replicated variables are never sent and a Server RPC on an unowned actor is dropped with no error — so trusting friends buys permission to skip *validation*, not authority. Skip anti-cheat; EAC's free price is not a reason to ship a kernel driver, support burden and Proton friction into a game with no competitive integrity to protect. **No documented position from Epic, Valve or any shipped post-mortem on how much authority a friends-only PvE game needs was found**; that is an open question, not an answered one. Bandwidth is almost certainly not the binding constraint — three upstream clients is a trivial fan-out — but the host's CPU and frame rate are, and the one concrete datapoint in the research is a four-player listen server at 144 fps whose connections "seem to get saturated" with warping until the host was clamped to 60 fps ([Epic forums](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/should-i-cap-fps-on-a-listen-server-and-what-are-the-recommended-network-settings-for-a-2-player-game/2666272)). **No Epic-published per-player bandwidth figure exists at any player count**; every number in that area is community-sourced and self-described as untested. Clamp the listen-server tick rate, cap host FPS, use `COND_OwnerOnly` for all per-player state, and profile before tuning. A design that spawns hundreds of replicated physics props would invalidate this conclusion entirely.

## The department store cannot teach four people a rule only one of them experiences

This is where the research contradicts a locked decision, and it does so from several directions at once. **State the collision plainly: the row that says the first level is the tutorial and there is no separate tutorial, narrator or training level is the row the evidence least supports.**

The research-grade foundation is Andersen, O'Rourke et al.'s CHI 2012 A/B test across three games and **over 45,000 players**, whose headline is that "the value of tutorials depends greatly on the complexity of the game": in the most complex game tutorials raised play time by up to **29%** and player progress by up to **75%**, while in the two simpler games the effect on engagement was "surprisingly negligible," and the authors conclude that "investment in tutorials may not be justified for games with mechanics that can be discovered through experimentation" ([Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-impact-of-tutorials-on-games-of-varying-Andersen-O'Rourke/d4a00da8cab8c5b2777385a698b02edc69736bea); [PDF](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)). A game with relic valuation, a light/noise trade-off, a shared rising meter, monster behaviour rules and an extraction decision is unambiguously in the complex bucket — which is precisely where the gains appeared. The defensible position is not "no teaching" but "no *explicit, separate* tutorial," with the building doing an explicit tutorial's job. One finding genuinely helps: **"Tutorial freedom did not affect player behavior."** Locking players down bought nothing measurable, which removes any worry about letting four people wander a department store rather than funnelling them. Gate the store for pacing and drama, not to force a curriculum.

The sharpest empirical warning is the measured cost of implicit teaching. In Cao and Liu's 2022 pilot (N=47, two builds of an Android runner), implicit teaching was rated significantly **less boring** (2.50 vs 3.10, p = .002) and significantly **less helpful** (3.50 vs 4.19, p = .017), with no difference in playability, difficulty, enjoyment or performance — except on one mechanic, where medium-skilled players succeeded at **18.8% under implicit teaching versus 58.3% under explicit, p = .031** ([Heliyon, PMC9676530](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9676530/)). Implicit teaching cut discovery by roughly two-thirds for the mid-skill band while making the experience less boring. That is the trade this design is making, and the authors describe their own work as a pilot calling for formal follow-up. Pulling the other way, a 2020 MDPI study found tutorial presence positively affected flow and continuous-use intentions **specifically among non-expert players** ([MDPI 4(3):41](https://www.mdpi.com/2414-4088/4/3/41)) — found by title only, so effect sizes and N are unverified. The reconciling variable is the player, not the game, and a 1–4 lobby contains both populations at once, which argues for layered teaching rather than picking a side. Note also what does **not** exist: there are **no published tutorial skip-rate figures and no first-session completion benchmarks** for PC co-op or extraction games, academic or industry. Any percentage in that shape would be fabrication.

**The retrofit pattern is the strongest single piece of evidence in this brief, and it names games.** Hunt: Showdown shipped a full onboarding overhaul in **Update 1.12, March 2023 — roughly four years after early access** — with a guided Basic Tutorial covering "controls, Dark Sight and Clues, gunplay, monsters, and more" led by an NPC veteran, plus an unguided Advanced Tutorial "akin to an exam," and it was *superseding* an earlier, lighter 2019 attempt at non-intrusive corner hints ([Crytek](https://www.crytek.com/news/hunt-showdown-update-112-delivers-new-player-onboarding-tutorials-adds-questlines-with-all-new-rewards-and-more); [Update 6.0 wiki](https://huntshowdown.fandom.com/wiki/Update_6.0)). Escape from Tarkov added a fully voiced tutorial at **1.0 in November 2025**, covering movement, looting, ammo penetration, extraction and healing, framed by press as a response to the Steam launch's influx ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/mikestubbs/2025/11/15/escape-from-tarkov-10-patch-notes-bring-new-guns-story-and-more/); [DTG Reviews](https://www.dtgre.com/2025/11/tarkov-1-0-beginner-guide-how-to-survive-first-raids.html)). Deep Rock Galactic runs an optional replayable tutorial *plus* a gated 10-mission assignment that must be cleared before the planet's regions open — which guides describe as the de facto tutorial. Phasmophobia's developers acknowledged theirs was weak and said they planned a rework; whether it shipped is unverified. Darktide's players state the consequence directly: "people are confused from the get-go, and then they get disinterested" ([Fatshark Forums](https://forums.fatsharkgames.com/t/darktide-is-a-tale-of-woe/120184)). The minimal-onboarding cohort is the inverse and the comparison is instructive: Content Warning is described as having "almost no learning curve," which is the honest reason it gets away with nothing ([gamecritix](https://gamecritix.co.uk/content-warning-review/)). **And R.E.P.O. — a game as loose as Lethal Company — built a tutorial anyway**, specifically because its hardest-to-infer element is a physics handling skill ([VICE](https://www.vice.com/en/article/r-e-p-o-is-6-player-chaos-in-the-greatest-way-possible-and-i-cant-get-enough/)). For a paid game, shipping with nothing and patching later means eating review damage at exactly the moment the review score determines commercial life, and the Hunt timeline is a five-year cautionary gap.

**The co-op teaching trap is the most specific design risk in this research, and it is structural.** Teaching a risk/reward trade-off — unlike teaching a control — requires the same player to observe both halves in the same beat. Portal's developers found testers grasped portals faster when they "caught a glimpse of themselves through a portal," so the team "deliberately positioned this first portal" ([Portal developer commentary](https://theportalwiki.com/w/index.php?title=Portal_developer_commentary&mobileaction=toggle_view_desktop)); the research-grade version is self-modeling, where watching your own avatar perform an action improved both performance and perceived ease of control versus no tutorial (CHI '26, N=60) ([TU/e](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/show-me-how-to-play-exploring-self-modeling-for-onboarding-in-vir/)). **In a four-player lobby the default outcome is that one player lights the lantern and a different player meets the monster, and neither learns the rule — each sees half of it.** The fix the sources imply is that the first teaching room must be one the squad passes through *together*, with cause and effect in a single sightline and no other novelty present; everything after that can be distributed. The order matters too, and the sources imply reward before cost: Mario's coins exist because "when they see a coin, it'll make them happy, and they'll want to try again" ([Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/3050823/super-marios-creator-reveals-the-design-secrets-of-its-famous-first-level)), and teaching the cost first in a horror game risks the player never lighting the lantern again, which forecloses the trade-off instead of establishing it. The canonical shipped precedent for the mechanic itself is Silent Hill's flashlight, which "draws monsters" while remaining useful for spotting items — "the tension of having to choose between seeing and fighting" ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-best-horror-game-weapon-is-a-flashlight-1828894342)). That is a design observation in a journalism piece, not developer testimony about how it was taught; **no GDC talk, book chapter or paper specifically on teaching a risk/reward trade-off was found at all.**

The most concrete tool the research offers is the Level Design Book's certainty ranking, which converts "teach by arrangement, light and sound" into a budget. Subtle aids (1–20% of players read them) include worldbuilding and architectural patterns, unusual detail, sound design, and **typology** — room structure implying its use. Coarse aids (35–60%) include composition and sightlines, lighting and colour, environmental storytelling, ground composition, and **in-world signage, which "players may mistake for set dressing."** Situational aids (70–93%) include scripted sequences, resource and breadcrumb trails, and **active threats**. Direct aids (95–98%) are always-on global UI and hard barriers ([Level Design Book: Wayfinding](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/wayfinding)). Anything every player must learn has to sit in the situational or direct bands. That yields a specific recipe for the lantern rule: pair a **coarse** aid for the reward (light revealing a relic) with a **situational** aid for the cost (a threat arriving *because* the light came on), in that order, in one room, with no other novelty, and then — per Kim Swift — reinforce it at least twice before combining it with anything ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/10-years-of-design-lessons-from-em-portal-em-s-kim-swift)). Portal's remediation precedent is the right instinct when a room overloads in playtest: "Playtesting revealed that this puzzle introduced too many new concepts at once... we inserted two test chambers before this one." The fix was more rooms, not more text.

The department store premise has a sourceable advantage and a sourceable liability. The advantage is real: **districts and typology**. Players arrive already knowing what a loading dock, a stockroom, a fitting room and a checkout are for, which converts the weakest teaching tier into something far stronger than it would be in an invented space, and the store's separable departments are an unusually good fit for Mike Stout's isolate-then-combine structure ([code.tutsplus](https://code.tutsplus.com/a-beginners-guide-to-designing-video-game-levels--cms-25662t)). The liability is Hodent's **false affordance** — "the perceived affordance differs from the intended functionality," as when Fortnite players harvested trees with the axe *despite feedback telling them not to*, because the tool looked like it was for that ([Hodent](https://celiahodent.com/gamers-brain-ux-onboarding/)). A store is full of objects whose real-world meanings will override the intended ones, and your teaching signs must compete with hundreds of decorative ones in a band where a third to two-thirds of players read signage as dressing. The Half-Life 2 composition rules are a direct constraint on four floors: **players don't look up unless something draws their eye**, players look where they're moving, and players respond to contrast in colour, shape, lighting and movement. Anything taught by a mezzanine or ceiling feature is missed by default. Tommy Norberg's three-way distinction between gate, one-way and point-of-no-return maps cleanly onto a sealed building's dramatic structure, and each is a teaching device as well as a pacing one ([Tommy Norberg](https://www.tommynorberg.com/leveldesigntechniques)). Also worth knowing: **Mario 1-1 was designed last**, after the harder levels ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-miyamoto-built-i-super-mario-bros-i-legendary-world-1-1)). If the store is the tutorial, build its interior difficulty first and author the entrance afterwards.

Three failure modes are documented and all three are live here. First, **implicit teaching does not fail loudly** — players do not report "I never learned this," they confidently report a wrong rule. Lethal Company's sanity mechanic is a light-exposure trade-off that shapes the behaviour of the game's most dangerous monster and was never explained, producing "seas of speculation about the game mechanics" ([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/lethal-company-sanity-explained-guide/)). A trade-off that is never surfaced does not become mysterious; it becomes folklore, and players optimise against an imagined rule. The only detection method in any source is Portal's: watch people play and ask them to tell it back ("At the end of a playtest we'd ask players to tell us the story back. If they couldn't tell us, that was a real sign that they weren't paying attention") ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/best-of-gdc-the-secrets-of-i-portal-i-s-huge-success)). For this game the equivalent question is "what were you trying to get, and what stopped you?" Second, **discovery teaches verbs and fails on economies** — the most consistent cross-source pattern in the whole brief. Content Warning's players lack shop-item information; Darktide's cannot decode brittleness, power, cleave or toughness regeneration; Tarkov's new tutorial specifically covers ammo penetration and looting. If one thing in Last Lightkeepers needs explicit teaching, the sources say it is **relic valuation and what a successful extraction is worth**. Third, and the single most important question the first minute must answer, is not mechanical: Phasmophobia's complaints are not about ghost-hunting technique, they are all about **"am I even doing what I'm supposed?"** ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/739630/discussions/0/3824173464653260948/)). That is goal legibility, and the tutorial-free literature is mostly about verbs.

Peer teaching does not rescue this. Social navigation — following the crowd — is a named strategy, and board-game practice holds that a veteran can teach by playing. But the three documented failure states are all present in this genre: the veteran outruns the newcomer (in The Outlast Trials, mixing confident and inexperienced players "can disrupt the pacing and leave newer players struggling" — [New Game Network](https://www.newgamenetwork.com/article/2753/the-outlast-trials-review/)), the lobby invents a wrong rule (Lethal Company), or the lobby leaves to read a wiki (Phasmophobia's information gap pushed players "toward outside wikis and YouTube"). "One veteran per lobby" is an unmanaged dependency, not a design, and **no quantitative or academic study of peer teaching in co-op games was found** — not its prevalence, not its effectiveness, not what happens when nobody knows. Hunt and Deep Rock both solved the unknown-lobby problem *outside* the level, with a per-account gate or a per-load warning. A single building that is the entire tutorial has no per-account gate, which means the same geometry must teach a four-stranger lobby and a four-veteran lobby. Hunt's lighter-weight pattern transfers better than its tutorial: **warnings that fire at the moment of a specific bad decision**, in the place where the decision is made — loading in without essential gear, with a damaged hunter, or without a teammate. That survives voice chat because it fires at the point of action. The hub lesson from Deep Rock, Hunt and Darktide is consistent and useful: **the hub is where re-teaching lives and the gate is where teaching happens**; Deep Rock's Space Rig holds the replayable Miner's Manual tutorial, and its hub is legible because it offers exactly one assignment. Darktide's hub, by contrast, still read as "padded" to a reviewer who had seen most of their other complaints fixed. Andersen's mixed result — "on-demand help harmed and helped player retention" — is the research-grade caution against assuming a reference library solves anything.

**The last collision is accessibility, and it is direct.** Microsoft's Xbox Accessibility Guidelines state the rule plainly: convey key information through **at least two channels**, because "cues drive gameplay, and if any cue is single-sense, players who can't use that sense lose critical information," and they name the exact anti-pattern — gunshots as the only warning to take cover ([XAG 103](https://devdocs.xbox.com/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/103)). A game that teaches through darkness, light and monster audio is teaching through precisely the two channels that must never carry information alone, and the audio channel is *additionally occupied by live voice chat* — not a disability issue but with the same effect of making the audio cue unreliable. Research with blind players finds game design "offering limited sets of unique sounds and often disregards their meaning and intuitiveness for someone who solely relies on audio" ([arXiv:2301.08031](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.08031)). The channels left standing are on-object visual state — something physically changing in the world — plus haptics, and the sourced techniques for using them are ground composition and contrast in shape and movement. There is a sourced irony to record: the accessibility guidance **recommends tutorials and training modes as accessibility features in themselves**, which means a deliberately tutorial-free design carries an accessibility cost independent of how good the environmental teaching is, because it removes the on-demand channel. Deep Rock's replayable manual tutorial is the available mitigation pattern. No study of accessibility in *tutorial-free* games specifically was found.

Four behavioural findings from the earlier Roblox research still apply here and are worth reusing rather than re-deriving, since they are platform-independent. **Loss aversion is real and scales with stake**: *Risking Treasure* (CHI PLAY 2020) found that "despite the temporary and digital nature of the game world... players still exhibited a strong bias towards avoiding losses," significantly more pronounced when more was at stake, and not varying much by age, gender, player type or gambling risk ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3410404.3414250)). The uncomfortable consequence for the lantern is that a player carrying a big haul becomes progressively *unwilling* to light it at all, so the greed loop can collapse into a stealth loop near the end of every run. **The Dredge rule** — Black Salt Games' stated principle that "the location of the player in the world should result from the choice players make in relation to their time resource" ([80.lv](https://origin.80.lv/articles/black-salt-games-on-the-development-of-dredge-the-pale-reach-dlc)) — means a position in the store must be earned by a choice about time, and anything that lets players be somewhere for free deletes the decision. **Dome Keeper's lift is the cleanest case study of a progression unlock deleting the game's central cost**: Thomas Habermann describes it as removing a "routine part of the loop," and separately says that without the wave constraint "you wouldn't really need to decide anything interesting" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/road-to-igf-2023/how-dome-keeper-focuses-on-systems-that-feed-into-one-another)). If anything bought after the price can shrink the lantern's cost, the late game loses the loop the early game taught. And **failure is not the churn signal; failure followed by abandonment is** — Roohi et al.'s analysis of 95,266 players found the pass-rate/churn correlation weak overall but strong when restricted to players who quit without finishing a level (Spearman's r = −0.586) ([arXiv:2008.12937](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.12937)). A wipe must end in something, which is the research justification for a recap that names what was secured. Also transferable: Lethal Company's escalating quota is criticised for having "no endpoint," and every well-sourced escalating threat is player-advanced and continuously readable at a glance — Dredge's eye, Risk of Rain's clock. A shared Storm Charge adds a failure mode no source covers: one player's greed spends the whole team's safety budget. Attribute it visibly — show who is lit and how much each lantern contributes — or the meter produces blame instead of debate.

## Two hours is a client clock, and half of a free demo's players quit in fifteen minutes

Steam's core refund rule is unchanged since 2015: Valve will "issue a refund for any reason, if the request is made within the required return period," which for games means under two hours of playtime and within fourteen days ([Steam Refund Policy](https://store.steampowered.com/steam_refunds/)). The researcher could not capture the policy as verbatim full text and the fragments quoted are the ones that returned as exact wording — pull the live page directly if exact text must appear anywhere public. The only substantive recent change is the **April 2024 amendment making Early Access and Advanced Access playtime count toward the two-hour limit**, which closed the loophole where players could log many hours pre-release and still refund at launch ([Lowyat](https://lowyat.net/2024/320997/steam-refunds-early-advanced-access)). For a game planning an EA launch, that matters: the EA buyer's two hours are the same two hours. No 2025 or 2026 policy change and no 2026 consumer-law ruling were found, though absence of evidence there may reflect search limits. The 2h/14d rule is a *guaranteed* refund, not the outer bound — Valve's page says you can ask anyway "and we'll take a look" — so two hours is a floor on exposure, not a ceiling.

**The clock is the Steam client's session clock, not a play clock, and for a co-op game that is the decisive detail.** Time in menus, lobbies, shader compilation and alt-tabbed idling all burns refund window. A first session that includes waiting for three friends to join can consume a meaningful fraction of two hours before anyone has played anything. It is also per-account and cumulative across sessions, so the design target is not "a good 90 minutes" but the player's cumulative first two hours of client time. Anything that consumes client time without delivering the loop — long installs and shader compiles, unskippable intros, lobby waits, EULA flows, menu tutorials — is drawn directly from that budget and should be measured as such.

The best refund benchmark available is GameDiscoverCo's survey of almost 150 developers reporting real rates: **median 9.5%, average 10.8%**, with most titles between 5% and 25%. Three cuts matter. By launch type, Early Access medians **12.4%** against 8% for 1.0. By review score, 95%+ positive games refund at 7.4% and 90–94% at 7.2%, while games **below the 80% Very Positive line average above 11%**. By average playtime, games over 50 hours refund at 7.4% and games **under two hours average playtime at 12.6%** ([GameDiscoverCo](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/steam-refunds-how-many-should-you)). The genre tags closest to an extraction game structurally — Sandbox at **13.9%** and Base Building at **13.7%** — are the highest reported of any tag, against Visual Novel at 5.5% and Puzzle at 6.2%. The planning number for an EA co-op extraction launch is therefore **12–14%, not 9.5%**. Two consolations: refund rate is reducible by patching — one developer self-reported 25% at launch falling to 13.3% over a year of fixes — and **no evidence was found that Valve penalises, warns, or acts on a high refund rate**, which GameDiscoverCo's piece explicitly describes as absent. That is a negative finding, not a confirmed absence; Valve does not publish enforcement criteria. The review-score and playtime cuts are almost certainly the same phenomenon viewed twice: refund rate is a *lagging indicator of first-session quality*, not an independent lever.

**The single most load-bearing number for first-session design is Zukowski's demo benchmark: a median playtime of 14 minutes across roughly 130 self-reported games, averaging 19.75 minutes, with a bottom-30th-percentile of 10 minutes and medians by eventual revenue tier running from 7 minutes to 65** ([How To Market A Game](https://howtomarketagame.com/2022/10/26/what-is-a-good-median-play-time-for-a-demo-benchmark/)). In a free demo, where the player has nothing to lose, half quit inside fifteen minutes. A paid game buys more patience but not an order of magnitude more. The gap between the top tier's 65 minutes and the bottom's 7 suggests first-session hold time is one of the few early signals that actually discriminates between outcomes — though those tiers are correlation, not causation, and must not be read as "make the first session 65 minutes long."

The derived budget is harsh and explicit, and it is an inference from adjacent data rather than a sourced industry rule — **no GDC talk, Valve guidance or developer survey puts numbers on "the first fifteen minutes" tied to the refund window.** The core loop must be legible within roughly the first ten to fifteen minutes, and the player must have made and resolved the extract-or-push-deeper decision at least once — ideally twice, so they feel both the greed payoff and the loss — before cumulative playtime reaches two hours. **A first level that defers the risky choice past hour two is a refund-window design error**, and the strength of the refund/playtime correlation means almost any front-loading of the core loop is defensible on refund grounds alone, independent of taste arguments about pacing. The first failure should also be pre-paid: Hunt grants a Tier 1 Hunter plus three additional pre-equipped hunters on first tutorial completion, and some of Mario 1-1's early pits have floors so the player experiences falling without losing a life. Teaching risk requires the player to be able to afford having taken it, which in a sealed department store means the first extraction must not be able to leave a squad unable to attempt a second.

The under-two-hour reviewer is structurally the most dangerous reviewer, because they are deciding whether to keep the game and whether to write about it at the same moment on the same evidence — **a failed first session produces both a refund and a negative review from one player**. Negative reviews are submitted after significantly less playtime than positive ones ([Gizmodo Australia](https://gizmodo.com.au/?p=1107051)), and a study reporting that negative reviews cluster under two hours was available only secondhand and should not be presented as hard. Review label thresholds are **community-reverse-engineered, not published by Valve** — the commonly cited table puts Very Positive at 80%+ with at least 50 reviews — and sales-per-review runs about 30 for games above 90% positive versus about 60 around 70%, so a well-reviewed game needs roughly 1,500 sales to reach 50 reviews where a mediocre one needs 3,000 ([Steam Page Analyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/steam-review-score-thresholds)). Score is not visibility, though: Zukowski documents well-made games stalling at "150ish reviews" despite very positive scores ([howtomarketagame](https://howtomarketagame.com/2024/07/23/when-mostly-positive-games-dont-sell/)).

Two gaps need stating rather than filling. **The co-op empty-lobby refund mode is unquantified** — no developer post-mortem attributing refunds to failed matchmaking or empty lobbies was found, and this was the weakest area of the research. What exists is qualitative: a Six Days in Fallujah player calling the game "almost unplayable for solo players" in the same breath as "Yeah, I have 14 days for refund" ([Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1548850/discussions/0/3808404524160557770)), and repeated requests for bots in thin-population co-op games. The private-lobby decision removes matchmaking latency as a refund cost entirely, which is a real advantage of the locked design — but it replaces it with "can I get three friends to buy and install this tonight," which nothing in this research measures. Relatedly, and importantly, **whether solo viability affects a co-op game's revenue is unanswered in either direction**. Lethal Company, DEVOUR, Demonologist and FOREWARNED all market a 1-player minimum, and single-player horror sells fine on its own — *No, I'm not a Human* passed **one million copies** at $14.99 ([Niche Gamer](https://nichegamer.com/no-im-not-a-human-sells-over-1-million-copies/)) — but no study, developer statement or dataset addresses whether a co-op game that is poor alone loses money. The mechanism is plausible (a solo-hostile game converts a failed first session directly into a refund rather than a retry) and it is a mechanism, not a finding. The practical read is that a playable-alone floor is cheap, every successful title in the genre pays for it, and it is the low-variance choice.

Instrumentation is the one area where a solo developer gets something genuinely free. **Steam Stats and Achievements measures against owners rather than sessions, which is exactly the denominator the refund question needs**: global achievement percentages are computed over owners and unlock once per account, so a hidden achievement fired on first launch, another on first completed run and another on first extraction gives a refund-relevant funnel with zero servers, zero cost and no new identifier — hence no GDPR consent problem, since Valve already holds the account ([Steamworks: Stats and Achievements](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/achievements)). Stats flagged aggregated get a global total with **daily history via `GetGlobalStatHistory`**. Its limits are equally clear: achievements are booleans without timestamps or ordering, aggregated stats are sums without per-player distributions, and there is no cohorting. The two load-bearing events are **decision-presented** and **decision-taken**: if owners never reach the first extract-or-push decision, the premise is undelivered and no balance work matters; if they reach it and always extract immediately, the risk curve is wrong. Steam's partner back end also surfaces **the written reasons players gave for refunds** — free qualitative data needing no instrumentation. Beyond that, Unreal's `IAnalyticsProvider` posting to a plain HTTPS endpoint, or PostHog's free tier, covers timestamped event sequences during playtests; Sentry's Unreal SDK is open-source and integrates with the default Crash Reporter, with the realistic line item being Sentry's Team plan at **$26/month billed annually** (50,000 errors, 1 GB attachments, 90-day lookback) ([Last9](https://last9.io/blog/sentry-pricing/); [getsentry/sentry-unreal](https://github.com/getsentry/sentry-unreal)). The games-specific budgeting risk is that crashes are correlated: one bad patch can burn a month's quota in an hour, which is exactly how the free 5,000-error tier breaks on launch day. Turn off minidump attachment storage by default — the attachment quota is the hidden cost, and a solo developer debugging with AI agents wants the symbolicated stack as text. Epic's own analytics documentation returned only a table of contents, so the `IAnalyticsProvider` detail above is unsourced; **no Valve documentation on what a developer must disclose on the store page about data collection was found either**, which is worth checking directly. The clean resolution to the unresolved legitimate-interest-versus-opt-in question in the privacy sources is to collect no persistent identifier at all: per-session random IDs, discarded after aggregation, sidestep the Article 6 debate at the cost of cohort analysis a solo developer with a few thousand players cannot usefully compute anyway.

**The project's own stated biggest risk — never having playtested the loop — is confirmed as the biggest risk, and the research makes it more expensive than it looks.** Nielsen's five-user rule applies to *finding* usability problems, not measuring anything, and it is contested: simulation analysis found five participants found about 85% of problems on average but with a range from nearly 100% down to **only 55%**, and concluded that ten participants reliably improved coverage ([Enfoque UTE](https://ingenieria.ute.edu.ec/enfoqueute/index.php/revista/article/view/126)). Multiple rounds of five, with fixes between rounds, is the defensible shape. But for a 1–4 co-op game **the unit of testing is the group, not the player**: five groups means twenty people, which is likely why co-op first-session problems survive to launch more often than single-player ones. Steam Playtest's Friend Invites feature exists precisely for this. At that scale the correct analysis is a per-player timeline read by hand next to the session recording — one row per tester, one column per funnel event — because the question is diagnostic and five data points establish nothing statistically. And since implicit teaching fails silently, the only detection method available is the moderated one: watch them play, then ask them to tell it back.

## Conclusion

The three locked decisions this research most strongly endorses are the ones that looked riskiest: PvE, private lobbies, and Unreal. Private four-player lobbies structurally immunise the game against both documented killers of paid extraction games — empty stranger queues and cheating — and the two titles that most resemble Last Lightkeepers are the genre's biggest commercial successes under a different label. UE5 earns its keep for exactly this game's two hardest requirements, dynamic lighting and replication in the box, and costs nothing in fees at any plausible outcome. What the research reframes is where the risk actually sits. It is not the platform, the engine or the genre; it is **three places where the current plan spends something it has not budgeted for**: a price not yet chosen in a genre whose evidence clusters hard at $9.99, a minimum spec silently set by a Beta lighting feature and a deprecated software path, and a teaching plan that asks four people to each learn half of a rule.

The teaching collision is the one that should change work this month, because it is the only one that cannot be fixed after launch without eating review damage at the moment the score decides the game's commercial life. The retrofit pattern is unanimous: every systemically complex game in this cohort added onboarding later, under pressure, and none removed it. The cheapest version of taking that seriously is not building a tutorial level — it is accepting three constraints on the store that the research supports directly. The first room the squad enters must put cause and effect in one sightline, with the group together and nothing else novel present, because a trade-off learned in halves is not learned. The relic economy needs explicit teaching even if nothing else does, because discovery teaches verbs and fails on value. And the extract-or-push decision has to be reachable and resolvable twice inside two hours of client time, because the refund window is not a marketing problem to be managed later — it is a design constraint on the first fifteen minutes, and it is the same constraint as the review score. The loop has never been playtested; the research cannot tell you whether it works, only that five groups is twenty people, that the failure will be silent, and that the only way to hear it is to watch someone play and ask them to tell you back what they were doing.
