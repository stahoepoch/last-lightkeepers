# Teach the loop, then ship cadence relentlessly

Last Lightkeepers has picked the right genre, the right loss model and the right monetisation rule — the evidence supports all three locked decisions more strongly than it supports almost anything else in this report — and is behind on exactly two things that the research says actually kill games in this space: **teaching the loop in the first run, and sustaining content cadence after launch**. Roblox's commercial ceiling in this genre belongs to PvE co-op scavenging, not PvPvE extraction: *99 Nights in the Forest* peaked at **14,153,173 concurrent players** while the platform's best-known literal extraction shooter, *Project Delta*, has an all-time peak of **7,898** ([Rolimons](https://www.rolimons.com/game/79546208627805); [Rolimons](https://www.rolimons.com/game/7336302630)). The two Roblox titles that broke a million CCU both use the "lose the loadout, keep the ledger" rule that the relay bank already implements, and both sell almost nothing — *99 Nights* has one live gamepass at **199 Robux** ([Rolimons](https://www.rolimons.com/game/79546208627805)). Where the project is genuinely ahead is the persistent rebuilt hub: researchers could not find a single Roblox title in this genre with a hub that permanently evolves across runs, and the retention literature gives that structure a theoretical mechanism (accumulated switching cost) that nothing else in the design has ([arXiv:1702.08005](https://arxiv.org/html/1702.08005v1); [Tseng, JCMC 2015](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcc4.12141)). Where it is behind is first-run legibility, variety per run, and the un-instrumented loop — and where it is at existential risk is the thing that took *Dead Rails* from 1.48M concurrent to a 30-day peak of 26,667, a **98% fall**, which its own community blamed on update cadence and loop repetition ([Rolimons](https://www.rolimons.com/game/116495829188952); [Dead Rails Fandom](https://deadrails.fandom.com/f/p/4400000000000038530)). On run length, the design handoff's 6–10 minutes is closer to right than the collaborator's 8–15 or the code's 20-minute ceiling, but for a reason neither source gives: the metric to engineer is **runs completed per session, not minutes per run**.

## Roblox's million-CCU tier is PvE co-op, and the locked decisions already match it

The structural finding that should settle any lingering doubt about the PvE-only rule is that the Roblox hits in this space are not extraction shooters at all. Tier one is mass-market PvE co-op run-based scavenging — *99 Nights in the Forest* (30.18 billion visits, 14.15M all-time peak CCU, a 30-day peak still above **2.1 million** fifteen months later), *Dead Rails* (6.61 billion visits, 1.48M all-time peak), *A Dusty Trip* (roughly 1.4–2.4 billion visits) ([Rolimons 99N](https://www.rolimons.com/game/79546208627805); [Rolimons DR](https://www.rolimons.com/game/116495829188952); [earnaldo](https://earnaldo.com/blog/a-dusty-trip)). Tier two is the literal extraction tier, two to three orders of magnitude smaller: *Project Delta* at a 7,898 all-time peak, *Havoc* — paid access — at **2,375 all-time peak and about 27 players online** at the October 2026 snapshot ([Rolimons](https://www.rolimons.com/game/13927562399)). Roblox itself is seeding the mature end via its Incubator programme, including *Drifters* from Bungie co-founder Alexander Seropian, but as a 17+/18+ title ([IGN via Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/news/group/9869/view/711153447479543260)). The non-Roblox picture agrees about where approachability leads: ARC Raiders, built on Embark design director Virgil Watkins' explicit pillar that "one of the core pillars of the game is to be approachable. It is about respecting the players' time," hit **960,000 peak concurrent and 12.4 million units in ten weeks** ([Pocket Tactics](https://www.pockettactics.com/arc-raiders/interview-embark-studios); [Nexon/Businesswire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260112348007/en/Nexon-Reports-ARC-Raiders%C2%AE-Passes-12.4-Million-Unit-Milestone)).

PvE-only also removes two of the four documented killers of this genre outright. Yager named cheating as the reason *The Cycle: Frontier* bled players before its anti-cheat matured, and named financial non-viability as the reason it closed on 27 September 2023 ([GameSpot](https://gamespot.com/articles/dev-shuts-down-pvp-shooter-says-cheaters-are-to-blame/1100-6515539/); [PCGamesInsider](https://www.pcgamesinsider.biz/news/73897/yager-closing-down-the-cycle-frontier-later-this-year/)). Tarkov's $250 Unheard Edition — which bundled PvE access with extra storage, upgraded starting skills and higher Fence standing — produced a backlash Battlestate publicly apologised for and partly reversed, and in its own COO's poll **53.1% of roughly 17,000 voters wanted PvE to be free** ([Hitmarker](https://hitmarker.net/news/escape-from-tarkov-players-outraged-over-new-250-edition-featuring-pve-mode-903488); [Insider Gaming](https://insider-gaming.com/escape-from-tarkov-pve-free-or-deleted-nikita/)). A PvE game with no sellable power has nothing to cheat for and nothing to sell that breaks faith. Roblox DevForum sentiment reinforces the same line from the other direction: open PvP plus harsh loss is repeatedly described as community-poisoning, with spawn-camping "making it frustrating for the new player to start off" and permadeath PvP producing "a feeling of constant paranoia when alone" ([DevForum PvP vs NPC](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/which-is-better-pvp-or-player-vs-npc/379765); [DevForum permadeath](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/mitigating-the-frustration-of-permadeath-and-should-i-even-do-it/2057027)).

The honest counterweight: the single DevForum thread that argues PvE "gets boring pretty quickly" states its own condition — *"if the Computer difficulty remains the same difficulty every match"* ([DevForum PvP vs NPC](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/which-is-better-pvp-or-player-vs-npc/379765)). That is the whole risk of the locked PvE decision, and it is a variety problem, not a PvP problem. The commercial evidence outweighs the forum: *Deadly Company*, a Roblox Lethal Company analogue with a three-day quota, carries roughly **12,000 ratings at an aggregate near 80%** ([Roblox](https://www.roblox.com/games/17173911032/Deadly-Company)), and R.E.P.O. became the breakout co-op hauling hit of 2025–26 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R.E.P.O.)).

What the Roblox leaders do that Last Lightkeepers does not do yet, in order of how well-evidenced the practice is: they protect the persistent ledger explicitly and *market* that protection (Dead Rails' Bonds "once you have them, you can't lose them" — [Slyther Games](https://www.slythergames.com/2025/03/13/dead-rails-how-to-get-bonds/)); they pay partial credit for partial success (*99 Nights* pays Diamonds for reaching Night 50 even if you never see Night 99 — [rblxguide](https://rblxguide.com/games/99-nights-in-the-forest)); they checkpoint long runs so a failure is never a total loss (Dead Rails' eight fortified bases across 80,000 m — [Droid Gamers](https://www.droidgamers.com/guides/dead-rails-ending-guide/)); they scale payout to chosen difficulty at run-selection time (**10 Bonds for Normal, 20 for Nightmare** — [TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/roblox-dead-rails-bonds-farming-guide/)); and they run two-axis meta-progression where you buy a class with earned currency and then level that class through its own tasks — a layer *99 Nights* retrofitted post-launch as "The Classes Update" and now runs at 35+ classes ([Pocket Tactics](https://www.pockettactics.com/99-nights-in-the-forest-classes); [99nightsintheforest.wiki](https://99nightsintheforest.wiki/The_Classes_Update)). The relay bank is the project's answer to checkpointing, and a defensible one. The missing pieces are the *partial-credit payout for a failed run* and the *difficulty-scaled run selection*, both cheap, both proven on this platform.

Where Last Lightkeepers leads is narrower but real. No Roblox title in this genre was found with a hub that permanently evolves across runs — researchers looked and recorded the absence rather than claiming it ([research gap, roblox_competitors.md](https://www.rolimons.com/game/79546208627805)). Decoration of a base is already proven directly monetisable there (the Decorator Pass at 199 Robux), which is the closest available evidence that Lanternhold's lit-rooms-credited-by-name model will monetise ([oofwiki](https://oofwiki.com/99-nights-in-the-forest/guide)). And one clean rule beats a spreadsheet on a platform whose own developers insist the audience rejects complexity ([DevForum retention guide](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/for-developersstudios-why-your-game-is-deaddyingnot-retaining-players/2863241)).

## The lantern rule is textbook push-your-luck; the relay bank is the part that can quietly kill it

The best-documented implementations of greed mechanics share one shape, and Last Lightkeepers already has it: a single visible meter the player's own greed advances, a bust that destroys the un-banked haul but not permanent progress, and a banking act that costs the thing the player wants most. Dredge is the cleanest case because its designers explained their reasoning. Joel Mason chose time as the only scarcity axis because fuel management "felt like tedious busywork," and the team "still wanted players to feel the excitement of being caught out at night"; the panic meter is a large eye rendered permanently on the navigation bar; night is when the valuable fish appear; and creature attacks damage a *random part of the inventory* rather than the whole hold ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/trawling-in-the-deep-how-black-salt-games-made-spooky-fishing-rpg-i-dredge-i-); [Prima Games](https://primagames.com/featured/dredge-review-the-horror-at-fish-hook); [Checkpoint Gaming](https://checkpointgaming.net/features/2022/10/pax-aus-2022-highlight-dredge/)). Black Salt's most transferable rule is the one about position: *"we didn't want the water physics to move the boat, because the location of the player in the world should result from the choice players make in relation to their time resource"* ([80.lv](https://origin.80.lv/articles/black-salt-games-on-the-development-of-dredge-the-pale-reach-dlc)).

That principle is the single sharpest critique available of the relay bank. If banking is free, instant, or available anywhere, it is no longer a push-your-luck decision — it is auto-loot with extra steps, and the genre has a documented cascade for exactly that failure. NetEase's *Badlanders* removed stealth with high movement speeds and auto-collected loot, so that "nothing really means anything," which in turn made its insurance system pointless and hollowed out the economy ([Naavik](https://naavik.co/f2p-mobile/extraction-shooters/)). Call of Duty's DMZ is cited for the same drift into looter-shooter territory once stakes vanished ([games.gg](https://games.gg/it/news/why-most-studios-struggle-to-make-a-successful-extraction-shooter/)). **Pressure-test the relay bank by asking what else it silently kills**: a bank trip should cost a deliberate detour, Storm Charge, light exposure, or a share of the haul. The symmetrical warning applies too — Game Developer's Hunt analysis argues that when money and equipment become too easy to replace, "the attrition and tension that give losses their weight" is weakened, producing "negative feedback loops" that keep players grinding on losing streaks while the core tension hollows out ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/engaging-players-in-hunt-showdown)).

The strongest peer-reviewed finding in the entire research set is also the most directly load-bearing for the lantern. *Risking Treasure* (CHI PLAY 2020) built a Zelda-style game with 18 wager decision points and found that "despite the temporary and digital nature of the game world, and the virtual nature of the gold, players still exhibited a strong bias towards avoiding losses," and crucially that **loss aversion was significantly more pronounced when more was at stake** — an effect that did not vary substantially by age, gender, player type or gambling risk ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3410404.3414250)). Two consequences follow. First, the carried-loot-halved rule will produce the banking behaviour the design wants, because a half-loss is still a loss and players will feel it. Second, and less comfortably, a player carrying a big haul will become progressively *unwilling* to light the lantern at all — the greed loop can collapse into a stealth loop near the end of every run. The relay bank is the designed answer to that, which is another reason it must be a real trip rather than a safety valve. Note the limit honestly: no study was found comparing total loss to partial loss to no loss in any genre, no lambda coefficient was verified, and nothing tests loss aversion when the loss is *shared* by a squad. The partial-loss design is defensible by analogy and by Dredge's random-slot damage, not by data.

The Storm Charge has one named failure mode, and it has a named fix. Lethal Company's quota escalates against a fixed three-day window with no apparent maximum, and the sharpest critique of it is that the escalation "has no endpoint," with players "tasked with one-upping themselves until there's no possible way to complete the task" ([Unwinnable](https://unwinnable.com/2024/02/16/lethal-company-and-the-ethos-of-the-bad-job/); [GGRecon](https://www.ggrecon.com/guides/lethal-company-maximum-quota/)). The unkillable Warden *is* the terminus, which is better than Lethal Company's design, and the escalation-only-rises rule matches Risk of Rain's time-as-difficulty, whose whole function is to prevent farming ([Giant Bomb](https://www.giantbomb.com/games/3030-42529/)). What the evidence demands in exchange is legibility: every well-sourced escalating threat is **player-advanced and continuously readable at a glance** — Dredge's eye, Risk of Rain's clock, Lethal Company's quota — and decision quality collapses when the player cannot see how close the meter is to the next tier ([Prima Games](https://primagames.com/featured/dredge-review-the-horror-at-fish-hook)). A shared meter adds a failure mode no source covered but Deep Rock's co-op-first framing implies: **one player's greed spends the whole team's safety budget**, which without surfacing becomes a griefing vector. Attribute the meter visibly — show who is lit and how much each lantern is contributing — or the 2.5x-per-lantern rule will produce blame instead of debate. That is an inference, flagged as such.

One more transferable detail, and it is the cheapest win in this report. ARC Raiders' original downed-player camera lingered on the body and "kind of got demoralizing"; replacing it with a hard cut to black produced what Watkins describes as a psychological switch "where people's psychology around that moment went from dwelling on their defeat to like 'oh, it was a punctuation'" — and the team "almost did [it] by accident" ([Pocket Tactics](https://www.pockettactics.com/arc-raiders/interview-embark-studios)). The felt punishment of a wipe is substantially a presentation problem. A fast cut from wipe to a Lanternhold recap that names what was banked costs almost nothing and is better evidenced than any economic softener.

## Six to ten minutes wins on completion, not on attention span

The run-length conflict across the project's own sources — 6–10 in the handoff, a 20-minute ceiling in `src/shared/Config.luau`, 8–15 in the collaborator's audit — cannot be settled by asking which number retains better, because the only platform-matched dataset points the opposite way from the intuition and is confounded. GameAnalytics' 2025 Roblox benchmarks show retention rising monotonically with average session length and then flattening: **0–3 min sessions give 4.31% D1; 13–18 min give 10.24%; 19–24 min give 11.46% D1, 1.61% D7 and 1.8 sessions/day; 25+ min give the highest D7 at 1.82% but uneven monetisation**, and the report labels 0–6 minute games "fragile" ([2025 Roblox Benchmark Report](https://www.gameanalytics.com/reports/2025-roblox-report)).

| Avg. session | D1 | D7 | D30 | Sessions/day | Median ARPPU |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–3 min | 4.31% | 0.41% | 0.13% | 1.3 | $0.48 |
| 7–12 min | 7.91% | 0.90% | 0.24% | 1.0 | $0.77 |
| 13–18 min | 10.24% | 1.20% | 0.34% | 1.0 | $1.21 |
| 19–24 min | 11.46% | 1.61% | 0.46% | 1.8 | $1.71 |
| 25+ min | 10.76% | 1.82% | 0.50% | 2.2 | $2.12 |

Designing a 25-minute run to chase that table would be a cargo-cult reading of it. Average session length is an *outcome* of retention, not a cause: deltaDNA said as much about its own equivalent data in 2016, warning that games "probably do not have short sessions by design, but rather because of high churn rates," and calling the results "blurry" to interpret ([Game Developer / deltaDNA](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/how-first-session-length-impacts-game-performance)). The peer-reviewed work gives the usable lever instead: across roughly 112,000 F2P mobile users, **total rounds played and total playtime were the strongest predictors of retention**, machine learning beat crude decision-tree heuristics by at most 1.2 percentage points, and first-session data alone predicted long-run retention at only **0.613 accuracy** ([Drachen et al., AIIDE 2016](https://andersdrachen.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/07/aiide_rapid-prediction-player-1.pdf)).

So the recommendation is a target of **8–12 minutes door-to-door for a normal run, with the early extraction reachable in roughly 4–6 minutes, and the 20-minute figure retained strictly as a hard ceiling rather than a design target**. The reasoning is the composition: two completed runs plus hub time lands the average session in the 19–24 minute band — the healthiest row in the table — *by way of* the mechanism the peer-reviewed work actually endorses, completed rounds. A single 20-minute run reaches the same session length while producing half the completions and offering the player no natural stopping point at which to decide to go again. The handoff's 6–10 is nearly right and slightly tight; the audit's 8–15 is directionally fine at the bottom and wrong at the top, because a 15-minute normal run makes two-runs-per-session a 35-minute commitment that the platform's observed patience does not support. Two supporting signals: a Roblox ad-spend postmortem recorded purchased visits that were "mostly 5 minutes of playing and then leaving," and a reviewer in a live feedback thread told a developer their objectives took "far too long to complete," recommending the concrete fix of **scaling objective length by player count** ([DevForum flopped](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/reasons-my-game-flopped-is-it-impossible-without-bigger-advertisement/3477521); [DevForum single round](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/players-leaving-after-a-single-round/4664307)). A solo Galleria push and a four-person one should not take the same wall-clock time. Reconcile the three sources by writing **one** number into `Config.luau` as the target and keeping 20 minutes as the ceiling, then instrument runs-per-session as the KPI that decides whether the number was right.

The deeper extraction's 20-second wait is the right shape for a different reason: Dead Rails ends with a deliberate commitment moment — pulling the bridge lever starts an irreversible four-minute siege — and that is the most-cited "point of no return" structure in the Roblox tier ([Droid Gamers](https://www.droidgamers.com/guides/dead-rails-ending-guide/)). Twenty seconds is small by that standard. It can afford to bite harder.

## What their designers would do first: let the run teach, and never run out of runs

Asked what to build next, the designers whose reasoning is actually on the record would converge on two answers, and neither is a tutorial or a second zone.

The first is **context-sensitive teaching delivered inside the run, not a scripted first raid**. This is the best-evidenced question in the research set and the finding is deflationary. Andersen, O'Rourke et al.'s CHI 2012 A/B test across three games and **over 45,000 players** reported, in its own verified section headings, that "tutorials were only justified in the most complex game," that "context-sensitivity can improve engagement," that "tutorial freedom did not affect player behavior," and — awkwardly — that "on-demand help harmed and helped player retention"; the authors' design implication is that "investment in tutorials may not be justified for games with mechanics that can be discovered through experimentation" ([UW PDF](https://grail.cs.washington.edu/projects/game-abtesting/chi2012/chi2012.pdf)). The commonly quoted magnitudes for the complex game (Foldit, +29% play time, +75% progress) could not be verified by direct extraction in this research and should not be quoted. Counter-evidence exists and matters here: an MDPI study found tutorials improved flow and continued-use *intentions* specifically for **non-expert** players, which is exactly Roblox's audience ([MDPI MTI 4(3):41](https://www.mdpi.com/2414-4088/4/3/41)). The reconciliation both bodies of evidence support is just-in-time, at-the-moment-of-need instruction — not a front-loaded sequence, and not nothing.

That verdict has a direct bearing on the collaborator's audit. Its claim that a protected 4–6 minute "Familiarization Route" is the highest-return retention feature is **partially supported and probably overstated as a priority**. What the evidence supports is that the first run must be *completable inside the first session* and that its teaching must be contextual; Hunt: Showdown's challenge system is the specific cautionary tale against the small-team shortcut, criticised as a failed teaching device because challenges "reinforce how to play the game" while assuming the player already knows how, carry no added risk, gate behind a timer, and reward less than the time they cost — "feels like an afterthought" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/engaging-players-in-hunt-showdown)). A bespoke scripted route is also the most maintenance-expensive version of the idea for a one-person team. Three cheaper substitutes are better evidenced. ARC Raiders lowers the cost of learning by failure with free randomly-generated loadouts that let newcomers and just-wiped players re-enter immediately ([Mobalytics](https://mobalytics.gg/gamebase/reviews/arc-raiders-review)). Dredge makes the hard layer opt-in — Mason: "one of our core pillars, was that the fishing mini-games in Dredge must be optional" — resolving the core action automatically while skilled input improves or worsens the outcome ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/trawling-in-the-deep-how-black-salt-games-made-spooky-fishing-rpg-i-dredge-i-)). And Deep Rock Galactic turns specialisation into pedagogy: four hard-specialised classes that "must cooperate to survive the caves," under CEO Søren Lundgaard's "co-op first" motto — "for us co-op is the first feature we consider when designing and developing the game" ([Co-Optimus](https://www.co-optimus.com/editorial/1978/page/1/indie-ana-co-op-and-the-deep-rock-galactic-interview.html)). If each Lightkeeper holds a tool nobody else has, the other players deliver the tutorial for free, in the moment, forever — and a small team gets an onboarding system by writing class kits instead of tutorial scripts. One hard constraint from the sentiment research: put the opacity in the *world* (what is out there, where the relics are) and never in the *interface*. GTFO's deliberate opacity works only because a community exists to transmit the knowledge, and its matchmaking was originally Discord-gated, with a reported — uncorroborated — **80% of players never clearing the first level** ([Level Infinite](https://www.levelinfinite.com/news/the-appeal-of-a-challenge-gtfo-and-what-it-means-to-10-chambers/); [ROG](https://rog.asus.com/in/articles/gaming/scarcity-leads-to-brilliance-in-the-smash-hit-action-horror-game-gtfo/)). A new game has no such community.

The second answer, and the existential one, is **variety per run generated by systems rather than by authored content**. Content throughput is the killer that a PvE design does not escape. *Marauders* peaked at 14,332 Steam players, stopped updating after August 2024, and fell to roughly 60, with the diagnosis that "Marauders never really failed because of its concept… the failure was content depth and a development pace that could not sustain an early-access community" ([games.gg](https://games.gg/news/marauders-breaks-silence-steam-overhaul/)). On Roblox, the same force shows as decay rather than death: *Dead Rails* fell from a 1,476,198 all-time peak to a 30-day peak of 26,667, and its community blamed update cadence directly — "there was an inconsistency of 'weekly' updates that it turned into bi-weekly updates," "the game is a bit repetitive and slightly grinding so I can see why it's losing players" — while *99 Nights*, which kept shipping systemic content, still held a 30-day peak above 2.1 million ([Rolimons](https://www.rolimons.com/game/116495829188952); [Dead Rails Fandom](https://deadrails.fandom.com/f/p/4400000000000039498); [Rolimons](https://www.rolimons.com/game/79546208627805)). Genre churn makes it worse and is outside anyone's control: Dead Rails' community blamed *Grow a Garden* and *Steal a Brainrot*, completely unrelated genres, for pulling players and creators away ([Dead Rails Fandom](https://deadrails.fandom.com/f/p/4400000000000039360/r/4400000000000170170)).

This is where the audit is at its strongest. Its "raid directors" proposal — one small event per run, blackout, security gate, distant alarm, shutter opening — is **supported**, and supported by the best source in the set. Lundgaard describes Ghost Ship's approach as "procedural level design… a nice mix between hand-crafted components and code," which lets the studio control where particular cave types appear within a mission, keeping variety without costing mission legibility; the studio's early-development priority was **systems before content** ([Unreal Engine](https://www.unrealengine.com/en-US/developer-interviews/guns-gold-and-glory-in-the-caverns-of-deep-rock-galactic); [NME](https://www.nme.com/features/gaming-features/soren-lundgaard-ghost-ship-games-deep-rock-galactic-3586886)). For a one-person team, hand-authoring a library of Galleria events and letting code choose which two fire is strictly cheaper per unit of felt novelty than building the solar farm — and it directly answers the one sourced objection to PvE-only, that it goes flat when difficulty never varies. It also makes the gating of the solar farm, rail yard, works and city behind loop-validation the correct call, confirmed by Black Salt's scope discipline: "we knew that for one thing to happen, we might have to pull something away in another area" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/trawling-in-the-deep-how-black-salt-games-made-spooky-fishing-rpg-i-dredge-i-)).

The audit's third testable claim — that "stories per raid" drives retention more than feature count — is **not supported by anything in the evidence**. It is a plausible designer intuition with no dataset behind it; what the research supports is the narrower, mechanical version, that failed runs must end in something. Roohi et al., analysing per-level pass and churn rates from **95,266 players** of *Angry Birds Dream Blast*, found the correlation between pass rate and churn weak overall but **strong when restricted to players who quit without finishing the level (Spearman's r = −0.586)** ([arXiv:2008.12937](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2008.12937)). Failure is not the churn signal; **failure followed by abandonment** is. That is the research justification for the relay bank, for a partial-credit payout on a wipe, and for the Lanternhold recap screen — and it is a stronger argument for them than any of the greed-mechanic theory.

## Monetisation, the hub and relics: the locked rules hold, with one unstudied bet

The monetisation rule — sell belonging, identity and decoration; never sell power, revives, inventory capacity, loot odds, or any way to keep a haul after a wipe — survives contact with the evidence almost intact, and is stricter than the market requires. DevForum's accepted line is best captured by StonksMcHat: *"It's ok to sell anything for robux aslong as it's resonably obtainable through ingame gameplay alone,"* against Kostiskat's rejected line, *"If you sell stats or coins (that can be used for in-game advantage) for Robux, that's P2W"* ([DevForum monetisation](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/best-way-to-monetize-a-game-without-being-too-pay2win-or-greedy/3428955)). Note that gamepass *volume* is itself a complaint independent of power, and that the cosmetic line is disputed — one participant argued paid cosmetics that cannot be earned also count as pay-to-win. The genre-native flashpoints are precisely the SKUs the rule already bans: *Delta Force* drew sustained objection for selling weapon EXP tokens and better secure containers ([Steam discussions](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2507950/discussions/0/595135824845089991)), and the one clear pay-for-power item found anywhere in the Roblox tier is a Dead Rails pass raising sack capacity from 10 to 15 ([Namu Wiki](https://en.namu.wiki/w/Dead%20Rails)).

Two deliberate departures from market practice are worth naming because they cost money. Monetising the death moment is an established and apparently tolerated Roblox pattern — Dead Rails sells an in-run self-revive for Robux, with one player describing dying because they "had no Robux left to self-revive," and Deepwoken charges **1000 Robux** for a Depths revival ([Dead Rails Fandom](https://deadrails.fandom.com/f/t/Bonds); [Deepwoken wiki](https://deepwoken.fandom.com/f/p/4400000000000374076/r/4400000000001451656)). The locked rule forfeits that revenue. That forfeiture is defensible, not merely principled: every child-protection finding in the academic set concerns *purchase* pressure rather than unmonetised risk loops — the European Parliament's commissioned study found **23% of interviewed children had paid for loot boxes**, and the FTC's 2019 workshop record names grinding, appointment dynamics and pay-to-win as dark patterns of concern ([EP study](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/RegData/etudes/STUD/2020/652727/IPOL_STU(2020)652727_EN.pdf); [FTC transcript](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/videos/inside-game-unlocking-consumer-issues-surrounding-loot-boxes-session-2/ftc_loot_boxes_workshop_transcript_segment_2.pdf)). A game whose stake is purely in-game progress is not what regulators have evidence against — with one caveat worth heeding, since "appointment dynamics" is on that list and a shared hub rebuild could easily acquire them. Also do not go paid-access: Havoc's paywall plus its 16+ rating is specifically named as a player-base limiter, and on a platform whose discovery depends on volume of entries that is a worse trade than on Steam ([Project Delta wiki discussion](https://project-delta-a.fandom.com/f), single low-authority post).

The hub is the project's biggest genuine bet and the place where the research is thinnest in the most interesting way. The strongest relevant finding is a stage effect: a log analysis of **51,104 players** of the MMORPG *Fairyland Online* found achievement and individual progression predicted retention "for players at the initial to the advanced phases," while **social features became the most predictive of longevity only once players reached the level cap** — and, uncomfortably, that **playing with many non-friends was negatively associated** with retention ([arXiv:1702.08005](https://arxiv.org/html/1702.08005v1)). Mapped onto this design: the personal workshop corner carries D1–D7, and the shared lit-rooms layer is for the long tail — shipping only the shared layer risks weak early retention. Tseng et al. supply the mechanism, where interdependence produces a sense of community and a *relational switching cost* that predicts intention to keep playing, which is the best theoretical justification available for sunk collective investment that cannot be carried to another game ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcc4.12141)). Research on **collective progress bars** — in marketing campaigns, not games — found that highlighting the collective portion broadened participation, including among people less aligned with the goal ([PMC6813241](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6813241/)). But the specific thing Lanternhold does, **crediting contributions by name**, is unstudied: the nearest published work is a protocol with no results, and no evidence was found either way on visible attribution versus anonymous aggregate ([arXiv:2503.13041](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.13041)). Treat the named plinth as a designed bet, not an evidence-backed feature — and keep it cheap enough to be wrong about.

The non-negotiable constraint on the hub comes from DevForum, where consensus is that hubs are tolerated rather than loved: "the best lobbies are the ones where the player can interact with the game early on and have a short span of entertainment as they wait," oversized hubs "look empty as well as take the player ages to get around," and the opposite failure is real too — "players will find being in the lobby more fun than actually playing the game if the lobby has too much to do" ([DevForum lobbies](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/what-makes-a-lobby-interesting-to-players/364481)). One developer's retention collapsed because a player could not find "where to buy and sell" after the camera loaded outside the world ([DevForum retention](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/how-can-i-improve-my-games-retention/2481364)). Lanternhold's job is spending loot and launching the next run, in seconds, in sight of spawn. Anything that adds friction between "run ended" and "run started" works directly against the runs-per-session target. And relics-as-the-only-lore-vehicle is well aligned with the audience evidence, which assumes low reading tolerance and skipped tutorials — a plinth with a name on it is environmental storytelling, which is the right register ([DevForum retention guide](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/for-developersstudios-why-your-game-is-deaddyingnot-retaining-players/2863241)).

## Tooling: five cheap adoptions, one real risk reduction, and a long list to ignore

The toolchain picture in late 2026 is unusually clear-cut, because most of the frameworks a project this size would be tempted by are archived or stale, and the few live tools are the ones that require no runtime changes. Every repository figure below was read directly from the GitHub REST API on 21–22 October 2026.

| Adopt, in this order | Status | Why, and the real cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Rokit** + committed `rokit.toml` | live, 483 stars, pushed 2026-09-27 ([repo](https://github.com/rojo-rbx/rokit)) | Prerequisite for everything else; one command reproduces the toolchain on CI and a second machine. **Aftman is archived** ([repo](https://github.com/LPGhatguy/aftman)). Note Rokit's latest release is v1.2.0 from Sept 2025 — stable, not abandoned. |
| **StyLua** `--check` in CI | live, 2,321 stars, v2.6.0 on 2026-10-10 ([repo](https://github.com/JohnnyMorganz/StyLua)) | Mechanical, one commit of churn. |

Has `generate-roblox-std` and `--allow-warnings` ([CLI docs](https://kampfkarren.github.io/selene/cli/usage.html)). Expect an initial pass of allow-annotations. |

ProfileStore deserves one project-specific instruction. Its **default auto-save period is 300 seconds**, up from ProfileService's 30, which buys "roughly ten times fewer DataStore calls" ([docs](https://madstudioroblox.github.io/ProfileStore/)) — but five minutes of unsaved progress is unacceptable for a game whose entire premise is that a haul is the point. Force an explicit save at every relay bank deposit and at both extractions, and shorten the interval. This is also the right place to implement the disconnect rule: the design says disconnects are treated like a wipe (banked survives, carried is halved), and a server-authoritative, session-locked save is what makes that rule honest rather than exploitable. The flagged caveat: ProfileStore's last push is over a year old as of October 2026, which is acceptable for a single-file module with a stable API and preferable to the thriving long tail of near-zero-star session-locking wrappers, but it should be stated rather than glossed. **Lapis** is stale since Feb 2025, **Lyra** has real cross-player transactions and would matter if trading were ever added but "warns against using it where data loss would be catastrophic," and no canonical maintained **DataStore2** repository could be located at all ([Lapis](https://github.com/nezuo/lapis); [Lyra](https://github.com/paradoxum-games/lyra)).

The frameworks question answers itself. **Knit is archived**, and its own archival note is the clearest statement of why a typechecking-first project should not adopt a service framework: Luau and Studio intellisense changed the picture, "these changes put Knit at a significant disadvantage in regard to developer experience. Knit cannot fully benefit from types," and the note deliberately recommends **no** replacement, observing that service-like architecture is "easy to replicate, as ModuleScripts themselves can already work in this way out of the box" and that "writing a shallow wrapper around RemoteEvents et. al. is trivial" ([ARCHIVAL.md](https://github.com/Sleitnick/Knit/blob/main/ARCHIVAL.md)). This directly confirms the audit's technical advice not to migrate to an ECS mid-project — and sharpens it: **Matter is the wrong ECS to be warned about**, having not been pushed since 2024-12-31, while **jecs** is the live one at 475 stars and a 2026-10-17 push, worth watching only if enemy counts become a *measured* bottleneck ([Matter](https://github.com/matter-ecs/matter); [jecs](https://github.com/Ukendio/jecs)). Flamework requires TypeScript and is off the table. Networking serializers — **Red is archived**, Zap and Blink are live, ByteNet stale since Aug 2025 — buy per-packet size, which scales with packet rate times player count and is not a four-player co-op problem; the cheap improvement is Luau type signatures plus `assert`-based validation at the server boundary in the existing `Remotes.luau`, which also closes an exploit surface ([Red](https://github.com/red-blox/Red); [Zap](https://github.com/red-blox/zap); [Blink](https://github.com/1Axen/blink)). For utilities, **RbxUtil** is the right shape — à-la-carte modules, 470 stars, pushed 2026-08-11 — precisely because it is not a framework ([repo](https://github.com/Sleitnick/RbxUtil)).

Testing is the one place where the honest answer is "there is no good option, so do the cheap thing." **TestEZ is archived**, **Jest-Lua has not been pushed since 2024-12-23**, and **lutest is archived**; there is no vigorously maintained in-Studio runner ([TestEZ](https://github.com/Roblox/testez); [Jest-Lua](https://github.com/jsdotlua/jest-lua)). **Lune** is the most actively developed tool in the entire inventory (v0.11.1 released 2026-10-21) and is the right home for pure-logic tests of loot tables, enemy scaling, Storm Charge and lantern timers as pure functions, and run-state transitions ([repo](https://github.com/lune-org/lune)). Its hard limits are documented by the community runners built on it: no physics, no characters, no DataStores, no real networking or replication ([LuneTest](https://github.com/AustinBarikdar/LuneTest); [DevForum](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/cli-lune-test-a-local-luau-test-runner-run-and-test-roblox-code-locally/4658410)). Since the existing Node JSON invariant tests already encode the invariants, moving them to Lune is mostly about getting real Luau semantics — integer division, string formatting, table iteration order — rather than adding coverage. The genuine adoption cost is not tooling but a codebase change: service access must become injectable, "pass services into your module rather than fetching them at require time" ([simplified.media](https://simplified.media/guides/roblox-unit-testing)).

Two further specifics bear on the existing code. The exploit discipline for a loot game is architectural: remotes that accept item IDs from the client are trivially abused — "exploiters can fire your remote events directly and send any item IDs they want" — and the prescribed fix is to accept only item *keys* and look up the real asset on the server, which means pickup, bank credit, extraction success and relic grant must all be server-decided ([DevForum](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/exploiters-bypassing-my-scripts-and-stealing-ugcs/4446605)). Roblox's platform-level Server Authority makes the server "the single source of truth," and anyone with edit permissions on a place "will not be actioned against if we detect them using a modified client in that place only," so you may test your own anti-cheat ([DevForum Server Authority](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/full-release-ship-fair-and-competitive-games-with-server-authority/4727993); [DevForum](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/an-update-on-automated-action-against-modified-clients/3640609)) — but one developer's scepticism, that it "opens up the possibility of far more exploits outside of movement cheats," is worth holding. And for `EnemyService`, `GlimmerRig` and `WindowWalkerRig`, the community's consistent claim is that Humanoids and NPC replication cost far more than pathfinding — "Pathfinding is performant, NPCS aren't" — with the recommended architecture being one shared staggered think-loop at 5–10 Hz rather than a loop per NPC ([DevForum](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/pathfinding-is-actually-incredibly-performant/4706863); [skillsdirectory](https://www.skillsdirectory.com/skills/el4cteo-roblox-npc-ai)). Note that a Feb 2026 article contradicts this, calling pathfinding "one of the most expensive things you can do on a Roblox server," and that **no official Roblox benchmark or NPC budget figure exists at all** — community estimates range from 20 to 40 active NPCs per server and disagree with each other ([plugin article](https://roblox-studio-pathfinding-plugin.pages.dev/posts/roblox-studio-pathfinding-plugin/); [enderdevelopment](https://enderdevelopment.com/blog/en/roblox-npc-ai-pathfinding-smart-enemies/)).

### What to do first, and what the evidence does not establish

The ordering that falls out of all of the above, for one person plus agents: instrument the loop with AnalyticsService funnels before anything else, because every recommendation in this report is a hypothesis until runs-per-session and the enter-to-extract funnel exist — Roblox's own docs call an onboarding funnel "critical to understand how to improve your game's new user retention and session time," the dashboards take about 24 hours to aggregate, and the binding limit is **3 custom fields**, so choose them deliberately (suggested: lantern-minutes, extraction type, wipe cause) ([Roblox docs](https://create.roblox.com/docs/production/analytics/event-types); [funnel-events](https://github.com/Roblox/creator-docs/blob/main/content/en-us/production/analytics/funnel-events.md)). Log a parallel copy of critical events somewhere you control, because a DevForum bug report describes funnel names mismatching and user counts not matching logged data, with Roblox responding that it was exploring options with no timeline ([DevForum](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/analytic-service-error-funnel-events-not-displaying-properly-in-dashboard/3427256)). Then: the wipe-to-Lanternhold cut and a partial-credit payout; Rokit and the three static-analysis gates; ProfileStore with a forced save at bank and extraction; one legible attributed Storm Charge display; a cost on the relay bank trip; a library of hand-authored Galleria events with code choosing which fire; and only then the Familiarization Route, if the funnel says first-run completion is actually where players are lost. Ignore, for now: any ECS or service framework, any networking serializer, paid access, a second zone, and any in-Studio test framework.

Five things this research does **not** establish, which the report would be dishonest to smooth over. No partial-loss system has ever been studied against total loss or no loss, in any genre — the halved-carry rule is reasoned, not evidenced. No study tests whether loss feels different when a squad shares it. The "stories per raid" and "guided first raid" claims in the collaborator's audit have no supporting dataset, only designer intuition that happens to be plausible. No developer postmortem, interview or DevForum design thread was found for *any* Roblox title in this genre, which is the single biggest evidentiary hole in the whole set — the most analytics-flavoured source available, GameAnalytics' Dead Rails piece, contains no retention, session-length or monetisation data at all, and its claim that Dead Rails "generates millions of dollars in revenue monthly" has no figure, method or source behind it ([GameAnalytics](https://www.gameanalytics.com/blog/dead-rails-and-the-hit-makers-formula)). And direct Roblox *player* voice is essentially absent: Reddit was inaccessible to the research tooling, so every "what players want" finding here other than the Deadly Company and R.E.P.O. market evidence is developers speaking *about* players. Treat tracker numbers accordingly too — Rolimons, RoMonitor, RoWatcher and BloxQuiz disagree systematically, partly because popular games run multiple place IDs (Project Delta has at least three), so every figure above is attributed to its tracker and dated rather than presented as fact.

## Conclusion

The most useful thing this evidence does is reclassify the project's open questions. Run length, loss severity and PvE-versus-PvP all read like design decisions and are mostly already settled — by the locked rules, by the Roblox tier the game is aiming at, and by the fact that the one number worth engineering is runs completed per session rather than minutes per run. What remains genuinely undecided is cheaper to build and harder to get right: whether a wipe ends in something, whether the Storm Charge is legible enough to argue about, whether the relay bank costs anything, and whether a one-person team can manufacture novelty from systems faster than players consume it. Dead Rails reached 1.48 million concurrent players and still lost 98% of that peak. The failure mode in this genre is not launching badly; it is holding badly, and the only documented difference between the title that held and the title that did not was content cadence.

The second reframing is about where the risk has migrated. By going PvE-only and refusing to sell power, Last Lightkeepers has structurally deleted the two causes that studios themselves named when their extraction games died — cheating destroying trust, and monetisation breaking faith. That is not a small achievement and it should be stated plainly, including in marketing. But it concentrates all remaining risk on throughput and legibility, which are precisely the two places a solo developer with AI agents is weakest and strongest respectively: weakest at sustained content volume, strongest at cheap systemic variation and instrumentation. That argues for a specific posture — treat the Galleria as a system to be varied rather than a level to be expanded, treat every authored asset as something code should be able to rearrange, and treat the Warden, the lantern and the rising meter as the three things that must be readable at a glance before anything else gets built. ARC Raiders' most valuable lesson cost its team almost nothing: they changed a camera, and defeat became punctuation.
