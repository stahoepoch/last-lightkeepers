# Buy the shell, build the machines

Spend about **$231 on Fab this week** and the art burden stops being existential. Two of the three things you need most are discounted right now — the entire Dekogon Studios catalogue is at **-30%** and the one confirmed UE 5.8-compatible modular retail shell is at **-50%** ([Fab search: Dekogon stores](https://www.fab.com/search?q=Dekogon%20stores), [Fab listing: Japanese Old Shopping Mall](https://www.fab.com/listings/1480726c-4a5f-4fd0-ab90-920e1f2abe10)) — and there is a harder reason to buy now than the sale: Fab's Personal tier is tested **at the moment of purchase**, and "**There is no need for upgrading from Personal to Professional tier if you cross the revenue threshold after the purchase**" ([Fab EULA](https://www.fab.com/eula)). Every pack bought below $100k trailing revenue is permanently licensed at roughly a third of the price it will cost after the game succeeds. Against that, three findings should stop you spending anything on the categories you are most anxious about. **A 1930s-to-mid-century American department store kit does not exist as a product at any price**, and neither does a single pack of reanimated retail machinery — your entire enemy roster is unbuyable, which is good news, because floor sweepers and stocking rigs are hierarchical transforms on simple bone chains, a programming problem your agents are suited to and a character artist is not. **Megascans is no longer free in full** despite near-universal belief otherwise; the free-to-everyone window closed on 31 December 2024 and what survives is a 1500+ asset starter set ([Epic forums, staff announcement](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/reminder-free-megascans-ends-soon/2203090)). And the licence risk that actually threatens this project is not in the build at all: **AI-training prohibitions are now near-universal boilerplate and they restrict a workflow step, not a shipped artefact**, so an AI-assisted pipeline can breach a licence with nothing ever reaching the cook. That needs a manifest field and a standing rule, this week, before the first pack is downloaded. One caveat coloured everything below when it was written: several primary licence pages could not be read, including the Fab EULA's Section 6 "Content Use Restrictions". **Two of those have since been read in a browser and the addendum at the end of this report supersedes the sections above where they disagree** — Section 6 holds no hidden shipping restriction, but it does forbid shipping a map editor or mod kit containing Fab content, and the AI-training framing below is narrower in the actual text than it is described here.

## Buy this week: $231 of Fab, $0 of software, and two reasons the clock matters

The buy list below is deliberately shaped around what your lighting reveals and what your design has already eliminated. First person locked means no third-person boom, no facial rig, no cinematic close-up — the bulk of what a character artist is normally hired for is already off the table. A 4-metre lantern radius means **texel-density mismatch, albedo-palette mismatch and silhouette-style mismatch between vendors are largely invisible**, which genuinely lowers the cost of mixing packs for this specific game. What darkness does *not* hide is scale error and specular/roughness mismatch: a counter at the wrong height reads instantly, and a prop that glints wrongly under a moving light is the single most conspicuous error available in a lantern game. ## Free and clean, versus free in memory only

The most expensive misconception available to you right now is that Megascans is free. It was, to everyone, under the Fab Standard License — "**but only until the end of 2024**" ([CG Channel](https://www.cgchannel.com/2024/10/epic-games-has-made-megascans-free-to-all-but-only-until-the-end-of-2024/)). What is permanently free from 1 January 2025 is a curated **1500+ asset starter set plus 5 tree packs and 11 scenes (both Unreal-only)** under the Fab Standard License, with the rest of the library now paid, including as themed bundles introduced in October 2026 ([Epic forums](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/megascans-starter-content-on-fab/2255322), [quixel.com](https://quixel.com/)). This still materially lowers your debris budget — **Brick Debris, Concrete Debris, Unfinished Building and Electrical Cabinet all currently list at Free on Fab**, as does an *Interior Debris Scan* ([Fab search: flooded water damaged interior debris](https://www.fab.com/search?q=flooded%20water%20damaged%20interior%20debris)) — but it means you cannot assume a surface or scan you remember as free still is, and every free Fab listing's **"License terms" field must be read individually**, because Fab now carries CC-BY listings alongside Standard ones and "Content under an open source license is not under the Fab Standard License" ([Fab EULA](https://www.fab.com/eula)).

The genuinely clean free pool is smaller than the internet believes and larger than you need. **Poly Haven is CC0 on everything** — HDRIs, textures and models — with no attribution required, commercial use allowed and redistribution permitted including bundling into products you sell ([polyhaven.com/license](https://polyhaven.com/license)). This is the strongest non-Epic source for this project: its concrete, tile, rust, peeling-paint and wet-surface library is the exact palette of a flooded retail interior, and because CC0 is not ShareAlike it mixes safely with Fab Standard content. Note the two carve-outs: Poly Haven's **site** content (logos, example renders, text) is not under the asset licence and needs written permission, and scraping requires permission. **Epic's Game Animation Sample — 500+ AAA-quality clips already on the UE5 Mannequin skeleton** — is the other standout, free and reportedly licensed for commercial use with Unreal only ([CG Channel](https://www.cgchannel.com/2024/06/get-500-free-game-ready-animations-for-use-in-unreal-engine), [Fab listing](https://www.fab.com/listings/ada3c1dd-c4c3-4e63-b4c6-bfd010b96ad7)). The UE-only restriction is irrelevant because nothing here leaves UE 5.8, and because your co-op stand-in body should be on the Epic skeleton, **the retargeting problem disappears entirely** — buy Epic-skeleton assets exclusively and IK Retargeter never enters the project. **MetaHuman** costs nothing beyond the standard Unreal licence ([metahuman.com/ai-info](https://www.metahuman.com/ai-info)), though for a first-person game with machine enemies it is a solution looking for a problem. **CMU mocap** permits inclusion in commercially sold products but not resale of the data, even converted ([CMU via Papers With Code](https://paperswithcode.com/dataset/cmu-motion-capture)); its quality and conversion burden make it uncompetitive against the Epic sample.

Three free things are traps rather than bargains. **AMASS and most academic successor datasets are non-commercial, explicitly**: the Max Planck licence permits only "non-commercial scientific research, non-commercial education, and non-commercial artistic projects" and states any commercial use "is prohibited" ([AMASS licence](https://amass.is.tue.mpg.de/license.html)). AMASS matters because it is the backing data for a great many research mocap and AI-animation tools — **any tool that does not state where its motion came from should be assumed to carry this restriction**. **Mixamo** is legally fine (royalty-free, commercial use, video games explicitly listed, no attribution, only raw-file redistribution barred — [Adobe Mixamo FAQ](https://community.adobe.com/t5/mixamo-discussions/mixamo-faq-licensing-royalties-ownership-eula-and-tos/m-p/13234775)) but is now an *operational* risk: that FAQ is dated 2022 and still says "technology preview", the service has suffered repeated multi-day outages with support staff reportedly calling it unsupported, and a July 2026 third-party guide describes it as in "maintenance mode" ([Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/free-character-animations-rigging)). If you want anything from it, **download it now and archive the FBX locally**; do not plan on the service existing in 2027. And **Unity Asset Store free content cannot be used in an Unreal game at all** — its EULA permits incorporation only into a Licensed Product built with Unity ([Unity Asset Store Terms and EULA](https://unity.com/legal/as-terms)). Fab itself carries Unity-targeted listings (*Low Poly Supermarket Food Pack* is flagged Unity-not-Unreal; an *Escalator - Ready to Unity HDRP* exists), so engine target is a per-listing check even on Fab.

Finally, two sources commonly cited as clean that this research **could not confirm**: ambientCG's own licence page returned 404 on two attempted URLs, and no primary Kenney licence text was read. Both are reported CC0 by a July 2026 secondary guide ([Cinevva](https://app.cinevva.com/guides/game-assets-guide)) and both are probably fine, but under your policy "probably fine" is not a filed grant. Verify in a browser before either enters the manifest — and note Kenney is stylistically wrong for this game regardless.

## What not to buy, starting with the $350 water simulator

The single most tempting wrong purchase on Fab is **Fluid Flux at $349.99**, featured on Fab's own front page with 166 ratings at 4.5 ([Fab homepage](https://www.fab.com/)). It is a simulation system for *flowing, dynamic* water. Your store is **still** water with a hard tide line, silt and algae, seen at 4 metres by a swinging lantern. A flat translucent plane with a good material, one horizontal decal band swept around the room at constant Z, plus the algae puddles and leak decals already on the buy list, will read better and costs under $20. That $350 is roughly one and a half times the entire environment buy list. The same logic disqualifies *Abandoned Airport* at $349.99 (no retail overlap), *The Mall - NGP* at $149.99, and Zhdanov's $109.99–$119.99 mall kits — all modern-mall aesthetics, a large spend for a shell the Befour pack covers at $19.99. **PropHaus *Small Town Stores* at $129.99** is the closest period-American product and still the wrong shape: it is a main-street storefront kit, exterior-led, several small shops, not one large floor plate.

Two purchases are disqualified by your lighting, and they announce themselves in their own titles. ***Meta Mall: VR Shopping & Commerce | Baked*** ($9.99) and ***SHOPPING MALL C BAKED*** ($7.99) have lighting cooked into textures or lightmaps ([Fab search](https://www.fab.com/search?q=shopping%20mall%20interior)). Baked ambient occlusion and baked light pools will be visible exactly where the lantern is not, which destroys the darkness the entire game depends on. **"BAKED" in a title is the clearest low-light disqualifier available from listing metadata, and the only one** — for every other pack, baked-lighting dependence was *not* verified, and it is the most important remaining pre-purchase check. One pack is disqualified on IP rather than on fitness: ***Resident Evil - RPD Boiler Room*** at $65.99 names a specific copyrighted commercial game in its title. The Fab Standard License cannot grant rights the seller does not hold in Capcom's IP. Do not buy it; do not ship it.

**KitBash3D is a double mismatch.** Its catalogue is exterior city and architectural kitbash — nothing addresses department store fixtures — and its Unlimited subscription rights lapse with the plan: "you can't use the assets in new projects after an Unlimited subscription expires" ([KitBash3D licences](https://kitbash3d.com/pages/licenses)). On a multi-year solo project that is a bad trade. If KitBash3D is ever used, a perpetual per-kit purchase is the only sane route. **Daz3D is the route to actively avoid**: its Section 3.0 Interactive Licence Addendum "applies only if you have purchased a Game Developer License or an Interactive License Add-On", and for content with multiple credited artists you need one **for every artist listed** ([Daz3D EULA](https://www.daz3d.com/eula)). Per-artist licences plus an obligation to prevent extraction from a shipped `.pak` is pure compliance burden with no upside when your humanoid need is one mannequin and one stand-in body. **AI 3D generation free tiers are out**: Meshy's and Tripo's free output is CC-BY-4.0 requiring visible attribution, two secondary sources read the free tiers as outright non-commercial, and the vendor docs and reviews directly contradict each other ([Meshy docs](https://docs.meshy.ai/en/webapp/pricing), [Tripo blog](https://www.tripo3d.ai/blog/commercial-use-ai-3d-models), [vantaige.io](https://vantaige.io/ai-tool/meshy-ai)). If AI generation is used at all, pay for the tier, keep the receipt and a dated copy of the licence page, and never ship free-tier output. Even then the vendor's grant protects you from the *vendor*, not from a rights-holder whose work was in an undisclosed training set.

**Humble asset bundles are good value for breadth and bad value for certainty**, which inverts under your manifest policy. The grant comes from each publisher's own EULA, not from Humble, and the **Humble-specific variants are typically more restrictive than the same publisher's direct-purchase licence**: Synty limits Humble copies to one seat and bars moving 'Best of Synty' Humble assets to other stores or people ([Synty One-Time Purchase Licence](https://syntystore.com/pages/one-time-purchase-licence)), and Animpic's Humble licence adds prohibitions on NFT/blockchain use and on "creating content for metaverse-related or game-creation software and products" ([Animpic licence](https://www.animpic.studio/license)) — that last clause is a live trap if a level editor or UGC feature is ever considered. Bundle keys also expire; one megabundle reportedly requires redemption by August 2027 ([ZerotoBeast](https://zerotobeast.com/humble-leartes-gigantic-game-dev-assets-tools-megabundle-deal-2026/)). The admin cost of filing 38 or 67 separate publisher EULAs can exceed the saving. Your own precedent proves the workflow is survivable but not free — the Function Loops bundle took a dedicated research pass to resolve and is recorded in `assets/audio/licences/function-loops-2026-10-28.md`. Buy bundles only when you want one specific publisher in them, and **redeem and download immediately on purchase**.

Finally, resist buying *variety* you cannot see. The research notes' "high build" at roughly $650 buys second and third fixture libraries so no two departments look alike. In a game where the player sees a 4-metre radius at a time, **that variety is worth far less than it would be in a lit game**. Spend the difference on the decal and signage layer, which is what the lantern actually reveals.

## Five machines nobody sells, and what they cost instead

Here is the honest hole, stated plainly: **nothing in your enemy roster can be bought.** Searches surfaced only generic sci-fi robots and drones — *Stylized Sci-Fi Robot Pack* (eight low-poly robots, rigged, basic idle/run/attack — [listing](https://www.fab.com/listings/30e12da2-8083-4afe-9563-0e2fcdc846f3)), *Sci-Fi Drones* (five characters, 22–50 animations each — [listing](https://www.fab.com/listings/2fd9ddbf-2887-48ff-8fb9-5b9fa50bec04)), *Sci-Fi Combat Drones* (nine drones, "ready for animation", **no animations included** — [listing](https://www.fab.com/listings/5d53f78a-c658-46de-8bb0-e021e0225de7)). **No pack of retail or supermarket machinery, shop-floor automation, stocking robots or planter-tending equipment exists, animated or otherwise.** "Automated retail machinery" is not a category anyone sells.

This is the finding that should reduce your anxiety rather than raise it, because **a humanoid rig is the wrong tool for four of your five machines, and that is good news.** A floor sweeper, a stocking rig, a loss-prevention unit and a planter-tending rig are wheels, tracks, telescoping masts, articulated arms and rotating brushes. None needs a biped skeleton, none needs Mixamo or MetaHuman, none needs IK Retargeting, and none needs a character artist. Their motion is **hierarchical transforms on a simple bone chain, or component rotation in Blueprint, plus Control Rig for an arm reach** — a programming problem, which is precisely what one developer plus coding agents is good at and precisely what a character artist is not. Rigging rigid machine parts involves no skin-weighting subtlety because rigid parts do not deform; this is the clearest "learn the skill" item on the list, a weekend in Control Rig rather than a hire. Near-darkness helps here more than anywhere else in the project: a machine lit only by a swinging lantern reads by **silhouette, motion cadence and sound**, and the telegraph WAVs already in your repo are doing more enemy-identity work than the meshes will. Low-poly stylised robot meshes that would look cheap in daylight are serviceable at two metres of lantern throw. The fifth machine, the display mannequin, is the cheapest to solve *well*: the *Mannequin Family Pack* is rigged to the Epic skeleton so every Epic-sample humanoid clip retargets directly, and a mannequin's stiff, jointed, expressionless nature means **low-fidelity animation reads as intentional rather than as a defect**.

If you reject all of that and hire, the numbers are these. A real December 2025 freelance posting offers **$1,200 per character model and $800 per character for textures** ([Work With Indies](https://www.workwithindies.com/careers/the-rhythm-company-3d-character-artist)), putting five hero machines at roughly **$10,000** — the largest single line item in the project and the one most worth attacking with kitbashing from bought industrial props first. Senior 3D character artists list at **$80–200/hour** ([Jobbers.io](https://www.jobbers.io/?p=75648)), with a vendor-blog range of $500–$5,000+ per character ([Juego Studio](https://juegostudio.com/blog/character-design-cost)) that should be read as the soft end of reliability. **Kitbashing machines is far more tractable for a non-artist than kitbashing faces**, so the order of attack is: silhouettes assembled from bought industrial meshes, then one hired modeller for the one or two heroes that assembly cannot carry.

Three other things cannot be bought, in descending order of how much they matter. **The lantern-holding first-person animation set** is first, and it is the single most likely place where "buy it" fails. Every purchasable first-person arms pack is built around a **two-handed firearm grip** — *Animated Modern Civilian Hands Pack* ([listing](https://www.fab.com/listings/2cae8375-f8de-4110-9079-8d5dccc21bf1)), the FPS Animation Pack family, large combat bundles — and **no pack advertising a one-handed carried light source, a carried crate, or hand-over-hand ladder climbing was found anywhere**. A permanently occupied hand whose motion *is* the game's lighting, plus one free hand for interaction, is bespoke. The realistic route is: buy a civilian-hands arms mesh for the look, buy a locomotion/interaction pack for the bulk of the motion, and **author the lantern poses yourself by editing bought clips in UE's Animation editor and Control Rig** — a tool-learning cost, not an artist hire, and a much lower skill bar than authoring a character. Hiring an animator for a focused 15–30 clip set is a low-four-figure job at the rates above. Second, **period signage and price cards**: Fab has no such product category, and these are far cheaper to author as decals and textures than to buy. They are also **the single highest-leverage thing a non-artist can do to make modern fixtures read as 1930s–1950s** — the store's period identity has to come from signage, because the geometry will never supply it. Third, **flood-specific authored content**: no pack covers silt deposits, tide lines or swollen floorboards. That the flood is a decal-and-material problem rather than a mesh problem is itself the good news.

## The AI-training clause can breach a licence with nothing in the build

This deserves to sit above the rest of the licence discipline, because it is the one trap shaped exactly like your project. **AI/ML-training prohibitions are now near-universal boilerplate across asset licences, and they restrict a workflow step rather than a shipped artefact.** Unity's Asset Store EULA §2.2.1.1(g) bars using Assets "for purposes such as training an artificial intelligence" without express consent, and Terms §3.8(v) goes further, barring use of Assets as "**inputs for artificial intelligence or machine learning model programs**", commercial or not, covering scraping and data sets ([Unity Asset Store Terms and EULA](https://unity.com/legal/as-terms)). Superhive vendor EULAs bar using assets "**or rendered images**" to "train, fine-tune, or develop machine learning or generative models" while explicitly allowing rendered outputs and compiled game builds ([Superhive product FAQ](https://superhivemarket.com/products/islands-nordic-archipelago/faq)). Adobe bars using Mixamo content to train ML models ([Mixamo FAQ](https://community.adobe.com/t5/mixamo-discussions/mixamo-faq-licensing-royalties-ownership-eula-and-tos/m-p/13234775)); Daz excludes using its content in AI systems to auto-generate derivative material ([Daz3D EULA](https://www.daz3d.com/eula)); the MetaHuman Creator agreement prohibits using MetaHumans to train AI/ML models ([Epic MHC EULA](https://www.unrealengine.com/eula/mhc?lang=en-US)); and the **Befour mall pack you are about to buy carries an explicit "Allows usage with AI: No" flag on its listing** ([Fab listing](https://www.fab.com/listings/1480726c-4a5f-4fd0-ab90-920e1f2abe10)). Fab implements this as an opt-in **NoAI meta tag**, which Epic documents as indicating "that an asset must not be used for generative AI data collection", and which is incompatible with Creative Commons so a NoAI asset must be offered under Standard ([Epic: Licenses and Pricing in Fab](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/fab/licenses-and-pricing-in-fab)).

Read the asymmetry. **Every one of these licences permits shipping the asset inside a sold game binary and forbids feeding it to a model.** Your cook can be perfectly compliant while your *process* is not. The breach vectors for a project built by one person plus AI agents are concrete and mundane: pasting a screenshot of a purchased kit into an agent conversation as visual reference; passing a `.uasset` or an FBX to a tool with a generative step; letting an agent read a pack's textures to "match the palette"; running a purchased mesh through an AI retopology, upscaling or texture-synthesis service; or asking a model to generate a variant "like this one" with the original attached. The Superhive "or rendered images" wording means **even a render of the asset is in scope** for some vendors. None of this touches the build. All of it is a licence breach.

The mitigation is three rules and one manifest field, and it costs nothing to adopt now. First, **add an explicit AI-use field to every row in `assets/MANIFEST.md`**, populated at promotion time from the listing's own metadata — on Fab that is the "Allows usage with AI" flag and the NoAI tag; elsewhere it is the EULA's training clause, captured in the filed grant under `assets/<kind>/licences/`. The field should record one of three states: *AI-input permitted*, *AI-input forbidden*, or *unconfirmed* — and *unconfirmed* must be treated as forbidden. Second, **make `Content/Vendor/` a no-AI zone by standing rule**: purchased source files, their textures and renders of them never enter an agent's context window, never get attached to a prompt, and never pass through a generative tool. This dovetails with the existing policy that `Content/` is yours exclusively and no agent opens a `.uasset` — that rule was written for a different reason and happens to be the correct mitigation, so **write the AI reason into it explicitly** so it survives future convenience. Third, when any generative tool *is* used, it operates only on your own masters or on CC0 input, and the output is treated as a thing you made: master kept outside the engine, filed and manifest-rowed like any other. Note the signal in the convergence itself — when Epic, Adobe, Daz, Unity and independent vendors all arrive at "no training" independently, that is the ecosystem telling you something about where generative 3D provenance comes from, and it is a reason to be sceptical of AI output for anything that defines the game's identity even where the licence permits it.

## Licence discipline: two Creative Commons licences, four platforms, and one unreadable section

Your existing policy is already stricter than most of the industry and it survives contact with this research intact. The refinements it needs are narrow.

**The Creative Commons allow-list is exactly two entries: CC0 and CC-BY.** CC0 "states no requirements" and costs nothing to administer; CC-BY permits commercial use, remixing and adaptation but requires that "credit must be given to you, the creator" ([Creative Commons](https://creativecommons.org/share-your-work/cclicenses/)). The ban-list is everything else, for three distinct reasons. **Anything with NC is out with no argument available** — a paid game is directly "intended for commercial advantage or monetary compensation", so BY-NC, BY-NC-SA and BY-NC-ND are unusable. **CC-BY-ND is out in practice** because ND permits no derivatives, and in a game pipeline retopology, LOD regeneration that alters the mesh, and texture repacking are all plausibly derivatives. **CC-BY-SA is the dangerous one and warrants a flat ban**, because its summary reads "commercial use allowed" and it is therefore easy to adopt by accident. Share-alike attaches to *adaptations*: an unmodified texture in a game is arguably a collection, but a retopologised mesh, a repacked ORM map or a material built on the asset is plausibly an adaptation that must itself be shared under BY-SA — and inside a cooked `.pak` you cannot practically segregate and offer that adaptation. Whether inclusion triggers share-alike beyond the asset itself is genuinely unresolved and no authoritative source settles it for games; the flat ban is risk management, not a legal conclusion. There is also a hard compatibility reason: **Fab Standard License content may not be combined with GPL, LGPL (except dynamic linking), or Creative Commons ShareAlike material** ([licenseware wiki](https://wiki.licenseware.io/wiki/fab-and-epic-content-licensing/)). One CC-BY-SA texture could put your entire Fab library out of compliance. **Keep GPL, static LGPL and CC-BY-SA out of the product entirely.**

CC-BY's obligation is dischargeable but only with process. Two things go wrong: credits get written at the end from memory, by which point nobody knows which rock came from which pack, and the credit must survive late asset churn. The fix follows directly from your existing manifest: **generate the credits screen and a shipped `THIRD-PARTY-NOTICES` file *from* `assets/MANIFEST.md` at build time**, not by hand at the end. Your manifest already exists precisely to answer "where did this come from"; making it the source of the attribution artefact means the obligation discharges itself.

On platforms, the research supports a clear ranking rather than a survey. **Fab is the safe default and should be the primary source**: the licence is platform-level, documented by Epic, tied to a revenue tier that is trivially evidenced, and the assets are already UE5-native, which removes a whole class of pipeline work. **CGTrader and TurboSquid are acceptable second choices with one mandatory check** — both impose a platform-wide royalty-free default with *editorial* as the carve-out, where editorial is applied when a model depicts another party's IP such as "a manufacturer's logo, trademark, or other protected IP such as ornamental designs in the geometry", editorial content "may not be used for any commercial, promotional, advertising or merchandising use", and crucially **editorial-labelled models may not be modified to remove the depicted IP** ([TurboSquid associated brands](https://www.turbosquid.com/help/en/articles/9937426-associated-brands-information), [licenseorg](https://licenseorg.com/compare/cgtrader-vs-turbosquid)). TurboSquid's own guidance notes that **unlabelled models can still contain depicted IP**, so the buyer must look anyway. **itch.io and ArtStation are per-seller lotteries, not platforms**: ArtStation's default agreement applies only "unless a custom agreement or license is provided by the seller" and is an agreement between buyer and seller ([ArtStation Marketplace agreement](https://www.artstation.com/marketplace/p/kqyB/jorgenhdri-interior-abandoned-factory-platform)), while itch.io licensing is entirely seller-set, with documented cases of packs carrying **no licence text at all** ([itch.io thread](https://itch.io/post/12381261)) and of sellers *changing* terms after sale ([itch.io post](https://itch.io/post/15952755)). Under your policy an itch.io pack with no licence text is unusable unless the seller confirms in writing — and then **the written reply is the grant and must be filed**. These are fine for a small number of deliberately chosen packs and terrible for volume buying.

The paper trail your policy already mandates needs five additions, all of which exist because vendors and platforms demonstrably change things. No platform checked publishes a retention period, so this is self-protection: keep everything permanently, past delisting, because the trigger for needing it — a DMCA claim or a storefront takedown — arrives years later. Beyond the invoice (vendor legal name, order ID, date, price, **licence tier purchased** — the tier matters because the files are identical across tiers on Fab), file: **the licence text as it stood on the purchase date**, as a self-contained PDF or HTML snapshot plus an archive.today URL, never a link to the live page; **the listing page itself**, because tier, editorial labels, NoAI flags and seller identity live on the listing rather than in the generic licence; **the pack version and file hashes** of exactly what was downloaded, so a later differing re-download can be distinguished from the licensed copy; and **any email where a term was clarified**. Two platform behaviours make the snapshot non-optional: Epic confirms migrated UE Marketplace products "might temporarily be available on Fab under the legacy UE Marketplace License", which Epic is phasing out ([Epic docs](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/fab/licenses-and-pricing-in-fab)), and Fab's own EULA says "**we do not guarantee that you will be able to always re-download or re-access Content**... We encourage you to backup Content you have purchased" ([Fab EULA](https://www.fab.com/eula)). Back up every pack to local storage on download; vendor account access is not a durable record.

Three traps deserve naming before money moves. The **embedded-distribution** requirement is universal and satisfied by normal UE cooking — Fab §4(c) grants distribution "as an included dependency" but requires you to "**restrict end users from extracting or otherwise using Content outside of the Project**" ([Fab EULA](https://www.fab.com/eula)). This is satisfied by assets inside a `.pak`; it would be **violated by shipping loose `.uasset` files or an unencrypted mod-authoring kit** containing Fab source assets. That is a live constraint if modding is ever considered, and it matches the condition your own audio precedent already records. The **"substantial portion"** trap is Unity's wording but worth internalising generally: §2.2.1(a) permits embedding an Asset only if it does not make up "a substantial portion" of the product ([Unity Asset Store Terms and EULA](https://unity.com/legal/as-terms)) — a game that essentially *is* one bought kit can breach a licence though every individual use is permitted. Your "repetition with intent" approach, with four kits per department and signage carrying the realism, happens to sit well clear of this. And **per-seat terms split by asset type, not by price**: art is typically licensed per-user across unlimited machines, while **editor extensions, scripts and code plugins are per-seat** — Fab code plugins are licensed per seat and a project including a UE plugin "may be distributed only as Engine Tools under the Unreal Engine EULA" ([licenseware wiki](https://wiki.licenseware.io/wiki/fab-and-epic-content-licensing/)). Irrelevant at one developer; a cost the day a contractor is added.

Now the part that must not be softened. **A number of primary licence pages could not be read in this research and are unverified**, because `fab.com/eula`, `unrealengine.com/eula/content`, `unrealengine.com/fabfreecontent`, `turbosquid.com/licensing`, `help.cgtrader.com`, `humblebundle.com` and Adobe/SideFX/Maxon storefronts returned **HTTP 403** or were otherwise unreachable, and `support.fab.com` returned TLS certificate errors. Specifically: **the Fab EULA's Section 6 "Content Use Restrictions" was never read** — the page truncated before it — and Sections 4(b) and 4(c) both say distribution rights are "subject to any applicable restrictions in Section 6". **That is the one remaining place a shipping restriction could hide, and it must be read in a browser before the first purchase.** Also unverified and requiring browser confirmation: the Epic Content License Agreement text governing Epic first-party samples, so shipping rights for Content Examples, Lyra, Valley of the Ancient and the UE5 Manny/Quinn meshes are **unconfirmed** — the City Sample and Paragon verdicts rest on a support reply relayed on forums ([Epic forums](https://forums.unrealengine.com/t/city-sample-in-eu-license-details/777090)), not on clause text; the ArtStation default grant for commercial game use; TurboSquid's and CGTrader's per-seat and AI-training terms; Humble's own representations; the licence designation of Megaplants and of every Fab listing currently marked Free; and the MetaHuman non-Unreal shipping permission, where a 2026 migration guide **directly contradicts** Epic's own AI-info page and three 2025 trade reports ([Stray Spark Studio](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/metahuman-2026-web-app-shutdown-migration-guide)). None of this blocks the $231 buy list — those are all Fab Standard listings bought at the correct tier — but **read Section 6 first**, and verify each item's "License terms" field reads "Standard License" rather than a Creative Commons or open-source licence at the point of purchase.

## Software: Blender, a bridge, and nothing else you will use

The honest software list is **Blender, free, acquired unconditionally**, plus one Blender-to-UE5 export bridge, and that is the entire required spend. What Blender solves for a non-artist is not modelling — it is **repair and conformance**: fixing scale and orientation on purchased FBX, splitting a kit into separate objects with correct pivots, authoring `UCX_` collision hulls, repacking texture channels, batch-exporting with consistent settings. All of that is mechanical, scriptable, and therefore exactly what an agent can do. Epic's **Send to Unreal** add-on is free but of unconfirmed currency for UE5 and recent Blender ([digitalproduction](https://digitalproduction.com/2020/07/30/blender-tools-fuer-unreal-engine/)); **BlendUnreal Toolkit** is the most UE5-specific paid option found, handling "correct axes and scale, skeletal mesh and animation split, LOD chain, auto-tagged collision, and ORM-packed textures" over a local connection ([Superhive: BlendUnreal](https://superhivemarket.com/products/blendunreal/docs)), price not captured. Try the free one against your exact Blender and UE 5.8 versions first; buy the paid one only when the free one breaks.

**Substance 3D is the only paid package with a real argument, and it is conditional.** What it genuinely solves here is making bought kits *match* — consistent roughness and albedo range across packs from different sellers — plus fixing a texture set that arrived in the wrong channel packing. Sampler and Painter's smart-material presets are where a non-artist gets value; Designer's procedural graph authoring is where he gets least. At **$59.99/month** for the Collection it is poor value for intermittent use ([design-offset](https://design-offset.com/adobe-substance-3d-pricing), after Adobe's rise from $49.99 effective March 2025 — [Adobe FAQ](https://helpx.adobe.com/no/substance-3d/pricing-change.html)); a **Substance 3D Indie** subscription exists on Steam reported at ¥2,800/month, with **no USD figure found** — the single most important unconfirmed price here, since it determines affordability. Budget it as deliberate blocks of months around texture-consolidation passes, never monthly-rolling across a multi-year project.

The "never" list is short and the reasons are blunt. **ZBrush at $430.80/year annually billed, or $54/month monthly** ([tooltrim](https://tooltrim.com/en/tool/zbrush)) is a sculpting instrument whose output is a direct function of sculpting skill you do not have, with no agent-driven path and no "fix my purchased asset" workflow — skip entirely. **Marvelous Designer** is garment patternmaking, relevant only if authoring characters with bespoke clothing, which you are not. **Houdini Indie at $299/year** (figures conflict at $269–$299; SideFX's own page unreachable — [SuperRenders](https://superrendersfarm.com/article/what-3d-software-costs-2026)) is the most agent-scriptable DCC on the list and the most interesting temptation, but UE 5.8 already ships PCG for procedural placement and Niagara for VFX, and Houdini's learning curve is the steepest of anything here; revisit only against a specific named problem UE5 cannot express. **RizomUV at ~$149** is a UV application, and you are not authoring geometry so you are not authoring UVs — purchased assets arrive unwrapped, and Blender covers repacking. **Marmoset Toolbag at $399 perpetual** ([Marmoset shop](https://marmoset.co/shop/tb5-individual-perpetual/)) is a look-development and portfolio-rendering tool; UE5 is your renderer and you are not selling assets. **InstaMAT** is a second texturing suite when the first is already marginal. The one interesting "no" is **Plasticity at ~$150 one-time**: CAD-style solid modelling is genuinely more approachable than polygon modelling for boxy mechanical shapes — which is to say, for exactly the machine silhouettes you cannot buy. Classify it as optional and revisit *only* against a concrete written list of hero machine parts that assembly from bought industrial props failed to deliver. And **do not buy a tool for anything UE5 already covers**: LOD generation, Nanite, material authoring and instancing, procedural placement, VFX, collision auto-convex, texture compression and look-dev.

On agent tooling, the research is clear and slightly contrarian: **a Blender MCP server is not required, and headless scripts are the better bridge.** Several MCP servers exist and work — the `ahujasid/blender-mcp` reference implementation, HTTP variants exposing "50+ powerful tools", a combined `tahooki/unreal-blender-mcp` ([unreal-blender-mcp README](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/@tahooki/unreal-blender-mcp/blob/9266c70cf61cc7e8090949cbbc5df6fbb235e2b0/README.md)) — but real-world reports put the quality ceiling at "not perfectly, but recognizably" right ([MindStudio review](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/claude-blender-mcp-real-world-performance)), one fork documents that **assigning multiple material slots only fills slot 0** ([Glama listing](https://glama.ai/mcp/servers/bxl52zucy9)), and the `execute_blender_code` tool runs arbitrary Python, with one reviewer arguing that a safe mode running in the same interpreter as the code it restricts is not a security boundary ([Strayspark](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/is-blender-mcp-safe-security-guide-2026)). `blender --background --python script.py` is deterministic, reviewable in version control, re-runnable and has no session state — strictly more auditable for batch work. **Use headless `bpy` scripts as the primary bridge; add an MCP server only for interactive inspection.** And because these scripts will be pointed at downloaded, untrusted asset archives, keep each pack in its own empty directory, keep scripts in a different directory, pass asset paths as arguments, and never run the interpreter from inside an asset directory.

## The import pipeline, and where Codex takes over

The discipline that makes purchased kits survivable has nine steps, and the first two are the ones that protect your licence policy rather than your frame rate. **Quarantine every pack on arrival** into `Content/Vendor/<VendorName>_<PackName>_<Version>/`, untouched, never referenced from a level — Tom Looman's advice to keep the game folder separate from marketplace packs exists so a pack can be identified, audited, re-licensed or removed later ([Tom Looman: UE Naming Convention Guide](https://tomlooman.com/unreal-engine-naming-convention-guide/)), and under your policy **the folder path becomes the licence key**. Then **promote, don't use in place**: migrate only what you actually ship into `Content/<Game>/Art/...`, renamed to convention. Anything left in `Content/Vendor/` is by definition not shipped, which lets you exclude it from the cook and keeps the manifest honest and small enough to verify by hand. **Promotion is the natural chokepoint, so record the manifest row at promotion time, not at ship time** — source pack, vendor, purchase date, licence tier, licence snapshot path, AI-use state, and the promoted asset paths.

The rest follows standard practice with three project-specific choices. Conform units, orientation, pivots, object names and `UCX_` collision **in Blender by script before import**, because pre-import is what makes it batchable and post-import means clicking through the editor forever. Author simple convex hulls at **8–32 triangles** for props and primitives for anything the player only bumps into; never ship per-poly collision on an environment mesh, and be especially careful on Nanite meshes where the render mesh is dense and collision must be independent ([Strayspark: game-ready asset checklist](https://www.strayspark.studio/blog/game-ready-asset-checklist-blender-2026)). **Prefer Nanite over manual LOD chains for static environment geometry**, keeping manual LODs for skeletal meshes, masked and translucent materials, and very small screen-footprint meshes — but do not enable it blanket. Naming follows `SM_`/`SK_`/`M_`/`MI_`/`T_`/`BP_` with texture suffixes `_D`, `_N`, `_M`, `_R`, `_AO` and no whitespace, backslashes, symbols or Unicode anywhere ([Tom Looman](https://tomlooman.com/unreal-engine-naming-convention-guide/)).

Two steps matter more for this game than for a normal one. **Materials are where purchased kits cost the most and where your biggest win is.** A kit arrives with one unique material per asset; replace that with a small number of master materials plus many instances, so a roughness-range change, a wetness pass or a colour-grade to unify vendors is made once. For a game whose identity depends on consistent atmosphere across assets from several sellers lit by one moving light, **a master-material layer is not optimisation — it is the thing that makes bought assets look like one game**, and it is the single pass that both unifies vendors and sells the flood. **Texel density** is the other: pick a project-wide target and check each pack against it, because a crisp crate next to a blurry wall is the clearest "asset store game" symptom and the hardest to fix after layout is committed. Where a pack is far off, change the texture resolution via import setting, LOD bias or max-size rather than re-UVing — cheap and scriptable. No authoritative texels-per-metre figure was found in this research; set it from your own budget.

What goes wrong without this is not visual. It is that **the project becomes un-auditable, un-optimisable and un-licensable at once**: once vendor assets are mixed into game folders and renamed ad hoc you cannot answer "which pack did this mesh come from and what does its licence permit", which directly breaks your policy and is unrecoverable without re-importing every pack to diff against. Add name collisions and redirector chaos, collision as a silent performance cliff, hundreds of shader permutations with no global look control, and cook bloat where kit demo levels and blueprints keep unused 4K assets alive through reference chains.

**The handoff to Codex is the mechanical half, and it is sizeable.** Everything in `Tools/` that touches Blender belongs to him: the headless `bpy` conformance scripts (batch renaming to convention, `UCX_`/`UBX_` collision generation at a triangle budget, scale and unit conversion, pivot rules — base-centre for props, grid corner for modular pieces — and one fixed-flag export function applied to every object), the **kit audit script** that walks a purchased pack and reports polycount, UV-set count, material-slot count, missing UVs, non-manifold geometry and scale outliers, and the **manifest generator** that turns a promotion into a `assets/MANIFEST.md` row with its licence-snapshot path and AI-use field. Three things stay out of his lane. Material assignment and instancing happen **in UE5, not Blender** — partly because multi-slot assignment is documented as unreliable through the Blender bridge, and mostly because material instancing belongs engine-side. `Content/` remains yours exclusively, which is now also the AI-training mitigation rather than only a convention. And the judgement calls in a kit import — which pieces are hero assets, where the silhouette matters, which prop reads at a 4-metre lantern throw — cannot be delegated to a script or an agent, because the tool has no aesthetic judgement and the "recognizably right" ceiling is real.

## Conclusion

The shape of this problem is not what the anxiety assumes. The art burden here is not a modelling burden — it is a **conformance and bookkeeping** burden, and conformance and bookkeeping are the two things a developer with coding agents is unusually well equipped to automate. The three things that genuinely cannot be bought at any price (the lantern-hand animation set, the retail-machine silhouettes, the period signage layer) are all either programming problems, decal problems, or edit-an-existing-clip problems. Not one of them is a sculpting problem. The $231 on the table this week buys the half of the game that *is* purchasable, and the design decisions already locked — first person, machine enemies, near-darkness, repetition with intent — were each independently the decision that made the unpurchasable half tractable. Darkness is the largest cost saving in the project, and it is already banked.

Two things change how the licence question should be held going forward. First, **the risk has moved upstream of the build.** A decade of asset-licence anxiety has been about what ships; the clauses that now threaten a project like this one govern what you *show a model*, and they are breached in a chat window rather than in a cook. The manifest field and the no-AI-zone rule are cheap now and expensive to retrofit after fifty packs are in the tree, which makes this the one thing on the list worth doing before the first purchase rather than after. Second, **the unreadable pages are a real finding and not a footnote.** Fab's Section 6 is the only place left where a shipping restriction could hide, and the Epic Content License text governing every first-party sample you might harvest was never read at all. Both are a browser session and twenty minutes away. Do that before spending, and the rest of this report holds.

## Addendum, 2026-10-29: Section 6 read, and the one restriction nobody predicted

This section was written after an adversarial review of everything above. Two primary pages that the research could not reach have now been read in full in a browser, and the result does not fall the way either side expected. It supersedes the conclusion's verification list on these two points.

**Fab EULA Section 6 is now read, and it contains no hidden shipping restriction.** Sections 4(b) and 4(c) both defer to it, so this was the live risk, and it resolves clean: a game shipped as a cooked binary with Fab content inside it is squarely permitted. What Section 6 actually contains is four things already anticipated above — no reverse-engineering, no standalone resale, no circumventing proprietary notices, and the non-compatible-licence bar that names GPL, LGPL and **Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike** explicitly, which upgrades the CC-BY-SA flat ban from risk management to a directly cited clause ([Fab EULA §6(a)](https://www.fab.com/eula)).

The fifth thing was not anticipated by this report or by its reviewer. **§6(b)(iii) forbids allowing any third party to incorporate Content into their own products, and spells out the mechanism: you "may not make Content available in world- or level-editing tools or templates or other modeling tools that allow works to be exported."** The earlier sections guessed at a modding constraint from the embedded-distribution wording in §4(c) and got the shape roughly right by accident. The clause is blunter than that guess. It means **Last Lightkeepers can never ship a map editor, a mod kit, or user-generated-content tooling that exposes Fab assets**, whatever the packaging. This is less a licence trap than a product decision being made quietly, now, by the act of buying: a private-lobby co-op game plausibly wants community maps one day, and that door closes on any department store built from bought kits. It belongs in DECISIONS.md rather than being discovered in two years. Note the inversion for Epic's own content — the equivalent clause in the Epic Content License, §5(c)(iii), carries an explicit carve-out that the restriction "does not apply to UE-Only Content", so Epic first-party UE-only assets are *freer* for editor tooling than anything purchased.

**The Personal-tier claim survives verification, which the review did not expect.** The binding text supports what the non-binding summary says. §2(a) tests eligibility "at the time of the Transaction", and its only clawback runs the other way — if you buy a tier you were *not* eligible for, Epic may bill the difference. §7(a) then closes it: "Any Content you acquired (whether free or paid) prior to the modified terms will remain governed by the license terms applicable at the time when you acquired the Content." Buying below $100k trailing revenue is therefore permanently licensed at that price, and the earlier recommendation to buy the kit list before launch rather than near it stands on clause text rather than on a convenience summary.

**The AI-training section above is directionally right and materially overstated, and the exact text is more permissive than either this report or its reviewer guessed.** The operative definition is narrow. §6(b)(vii) with §16(l) restricts NoAI Content only as (a) data in a Generative AI Program's dataset, (b) input to its development, or (c) a training input — and §16(l)(i) then carves out, explicitly, programs that "solely operate on the original content", that "generate tags to classify visual input content", or that **"generate instructions to arrange existing content, without creating new content."** That last clause describes a procedural level builder almost exactly, and it means the mechanical work this project actually wants from its agents — conformance scripts, channel repacking, batch export, placement logic, manifest tagging — sits outside the definition rather than merely being tolerated by it. The three mitigations above are still worth adopting, because the manifest field costs nothing and *unconfirmed-means-forbidden* is the right default across a dozen vendors with a dozen wordings. But the standing rule should be stated as the narrow thing it is: **do not put purchased source files, textures, or renders of them into any service that may train on its inputs.** Not *no agent may ever reason about a bought asset*. The earlier framing would have banned work the licence permits.

**One asymmetry worth knowing, smaller than this report first claimed.** Fab §7(a) is a clean grandfather clause: acquired content keeps the terms it was acquired under, full stop. The Epic Content License — which governs Game Animation Sample, the first-party samples and the Megascans library — reaches the same place by a worse route. Its §7(b) does say that **declining an amendment leaves the prior agreement governing content you already have access to**; what it adds is that accepting one makes the new terms apply retroactively to everything already downloaded, and that declining blocks future downloads and some Epic services. An earlier draft of this addendum read that as Epic content being "retroactively re-termable in practice". That is too strong, and the correction is worth preserving: the old grant does not lapse by itself, so this is a practical annoyance — you may face a choice between keeping old terms and keeping launcher access — rather than a loss of rights. It argues for one cheap habit and nothing more: **archive a dated copy of the agreement text alongside the download**, exactly as the paper-trail rules above already require for paid packs, so that if the choice ever arrives you can tell which terms you are standing on.

Also now confirmed from clause text rather than inference: shipping rights for Epic first-party samples are real — §3(a) permits distributing Licensed Content in object code as an inseparable part of a Project, naming video games as the example — which removes the Content Examples / Lyra / Manny-Quinn uncertainty flagged in the conclusion. And the Megascans Personal plan has a grace the earlier section did not know about, in that an individual who starts on a personal plan "will not be ineligible for the plan unless and until they generate more than $100,000 USD in annual gross revenue **for two consecutive years**" — but also a restriction Fab does not impose: Megascans Personal content "may not be distributed in source format to anyone else", with no collaborator exception at all, where Fab §5(a) explicitly permits sharing with contractors working on the Project. The day a contractor is hired, Fab assets can be handed over and Megascans Personal assets cannot.

**What this addendum does not rescue.** Four objections to the sections above stand and should be read as corrections. The Dekogon catalogue discount is real, but "no discoverable end date" is an absence of evidence rather than a deadline, and with the Personal-tier lock-in now verified the sale is no longer the reason to act — so there is no same-day urgency on anything except the 3 November free-content rail. The Humble conclusion is only ever "no relevant live bundle could be verified", not a storefront finding. The Megascans free-tier claim is too broad to plan an environment around and should be treated as unquantified until the library is opened. And the five back-of-house listings in the buy table are individually named and priced above, but any summary that collapses them into a single "industrial set" invents a product that does not exist; buy them as five decisions or not at all.

**The sequencing objection is the strongest thing the review raised, and it is right in a way this report missed.** The Befour kit's identity-bearing dressing is Japanese, and the earlier sections already argue that period American signage is the unbuyable thing carrying the realism — so the kit is a shell, and its walls, ceilings, glass and decay are what the $19.99 buys. But a code-built scaffold already exists in this project and is free, tunable, and about to be walked. Two scaffolds is one too many. The honest ordering is therefore: claim the Epic animation sample now, because it is free and its skeleton decision shapes every later purchase; buy the Befour shell once the greybox has said what shape the floor should be; and buy exactly one broad retail-prop kit against departments that have been chosen, rather than four overlapping ones against departments that have not.
