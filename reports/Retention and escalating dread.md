# Escalate the procedure, not the volume

Chris's framework is more defensible than the retention data it is meant to serve, and the single sharpest finding in this research is that **his Storm Charge rule — a meter that only ever rises — contradicts every documented pacing system in the genre's canon.** Valve's Left 4 Dead Director, Creative Assembly's menace gauge and Resident Evil 4's original adaptive difficulty all implement the same inverted control law: when player pressure peaks, *remove threat*. Valve states it outright — "**Algorithm adjusts pacing, not difficulty — Amplitude (difficulty) is not changed, frequency (pacing) is**" ([Booth, Valve GDC 2009](https://steamcdn-a.akamaihd.net/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf)). That conflict is reconcilable, but only by separating what rises (the building's authority to act) from what oscillates (how much of that authority is spent per minute), and the report spends a section on exactly how. The rest of the evidence is harsher on the premises than on the design. **The median buyer of every comparable title churns between 6.8 and 16.2 hours**, which is to say right at the boundary this project is asking about ([PlayTracker](https://playtracker.net/insight/game/43868)). **Loss aversion — the loudest claim in extraction-genre design discourse — rests on a literature that describes itself as nearly empty.** Fear habituates physiologically *before players notice it has*, so playtest self-report will systematically overestimate how much dread survives run twenty. Containment-as-failure has no shipped precedent at all: it is the project's most original ground and its least validated. And private-only co-op makes the *group*, not the player, the unit of churn, with no repair path when one member drifts. Against that, four of Chris's five replayability pillars are well-supported, his four night states map almost exactly onto the one anti-habituation technique Creative Assembly documented, and his proposed next slice is the correct next slice.

## The median buyer leaves at hour twelve and no structure in this evidence stops it

Start with the number that reframes the whole question. Median total playtime across the closest comparables: **GTFO 6.8 hours, Lethal Company 12, Deep Rock Galactic 14.1, Phasmophobia 16.2** — against averages two to five times higher, which is the signature of a heavy-tail minority dragging the mean ([PlayTracker: GTFO](https://playtracker.net/insight/game/43868), [Lethal Company](https://playtracker.net/insight/game/89765), [DRG](https://playtracker.net/insight/game/8686), [Phasmophobia](https://playtracker.net/insight/game/62821)). Long-term retention in this genre is not a property of the player base; it is a property of a small fraction of it. Lethal Company — structurally the closest published analogue to Last Lightkeepers — fell from a **107,897 monthly average in December 2023 to 2,654 in September 2026, a 97.5% decline**, with roughly 89% of it inside six months ([SteamCharts](https://steamcharts.com/app/1966720)). Its Twitch average viewership halved over the same two months its concurrency halved ([TwitchTracker](https://twitchtracker.com/games/2085980140)), which is about as close as public data gets to saying the retention was the virality.

Ranked by peak retained in September 2026, the field splits cleanly and informatively: **Barotrauma 28.14%, Deep Rock Galactic 18.25%, Phasmophobia 16.03%, Risk of Rain 2 11.63%, GTFO 9.05%, Lethal Company 2.29%, Void Bastards ~1.29%** ([SteamCharts, per-title](https://steamcharts.com/app/602960)). The tempting reading — difficulty ladders retain, rising quotas don't — is half right and must be stated carefully, because **every title above 10% also shipped a continuous content pipeline, so structure and content are confounded in every surviving case.** What the extremes do establish is that the *absence* of a ladder looks close to fatal: Void Bastards had deep procedural variety, no ladder, and bottomed out at **8.76 average concurrent players** ([SteamCharts](https://steamcharts.com/app/857980)); Lethal Company has a monotonic quota, a thin moon-tier ladder, and sits at 2.29%. Last Lightkeepers' locked structure is, mechanically, Lethal Company's: a meter that only rises, no ending.

Which of the five candidate drivers actually carry hours twenty-plus? The evidence ranks them, and the ranking is unkind to the two the genre talks about most. A study of **51,104 players** in Fairyland Online found achievement dominant early and **social features becoming the most predictive of longevity at level cap** — the two drivers trade places across the lifecycle ([arXiv:1702.08005](https://arxiv.org/html/1702.08005v1)). A 2026 PLOS ONE paper found that combining friendship ties with **PvE teammate ties** improves churn prediction synergistically over either network alone, which is the closest thing to direct evidence that co-op partners are themselves the retention mechanism ([PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0356200)). Mastery is the other compounder, and Hunt: Showdown is the proof by contrast — the only title in the set with no secular decline, six years of growth then a five-year 14k–20k plateau, and the only one whose skill ceiling is set by other humans rather than authored content ([SteamCharts](https://steamcharts.com/app/594650)). Last Lightkeepers, being PvE-only, cannot buy that renewing ceiling; it has to author one.

Then the awkward part. **Fear is the best-attested acquisition driver and has no evidence whatsoever as a retainer.** Every Crytek statement about Hunt's tension concerns *generating* dread through information denial and audio, never sustaining it across hundreds of hours ([PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/2020/02/17/listen-closely-insight-into-the-sound-design-of-hunt-showdown/)), and the strongest structural counter-evidence is Lethal Company's own curve. **Loss aversion is thinner still.** A dedicated University of Saskatchewan thesis on the subject states plainly that "**very few empirical studies exist formally in the realm of loss aversion and game design**"; its own experiment confirmed the bias exists in a game context but measured *within-session decision behaviour*, not retention ([USask thesis](https://harvest.usask.ca/bitstream/handle/10388/13478/BANDEIRAROMAOTOME-THESIS-2021.pdf)). Orji et al., cited in the same work, found loss-based engagement effective only for *certain player types*. The 2:1 loss-to-gain figure that circulates in extraction-design journalism is itself contested in behavioural economics. There is no peer-reviewed study, no telemetry, and no A/B test anywhere comparing haul-loss tension against death tension. **Any design document for this project that rests on "risk of losing loot" as a retention mechanism is resting on folklore plus one thesis describing its own field as nearly empty.**

The defensible version of the claim is weaker and more useful: *tension scales with the size and visibility of what is at risk, and death is only a good carrier of tension when death destroys something.* That reframing is good news here, because it explains why removing death costs nothing provided the haul stays at risk. It also sets a hard requirement — loss aversion engages only once the player feels they *own* the haul, so ownership must transfer early and physically (named items, objects carried by hand, a visible tally), not resolve as a number at extraction. Tarkov is the live demonstration that stakes must be actively defended rather than assumed: Battlestate **lowered PvE loot levels in July 2024** because players found a route to risk-free looting and took it ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/mikestubbs/2024/07/11/escape-from-tarkov-update-changes-loot-spawns-which-now-suck/)).

One reframe before moving on, because it changes what success means. Lethal Company's 2.29% is a commercial triumph: roughly **10 million copies at ~$10** ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/lethal-company-sold-an-estimated-10-million-copies)). Percentage retention and commercial sufficiency are different questions, and a solo premium title should be optimising the second. **A monotonic-meter design should plan its economics around a steep decay curve as the expected outcome, not as a failure state** — and should spend its design budget on the units-sold window rather than on preventing a decay that nothing in this dataset prevents.

## Every documented pacing system relieves at peak pressure; the Storm Charge only rises

This is the hard conflict with a locked rule, and it needs stating without softening.

Valve's published slides give the most portable artefact in this entire body of work, and it is a four-player co-op system with real numbers. The Director estimates each Survivor's emotional intensity, **tracks the maximum across all four**, and "if intensity is too high, remove major threats for awhile." The state machine runs **Build Up → Sustain Peak (full threat population continues 3–5 seconds past the intensity peak) → Peak Fade (minimal population, waiting for intensity to decay, existing specifically so the Relax period cannot begin mid-engagement) → Relax (minimal population for 30–45 seconds)**. The rationale is explicit: "**Constant, unchanging combat is fatiguing**" and "Long periods of inactivity are boring," with "unpredictable peaks and valleys of intensity" named as the thing that creates a replayable experience ([Booth, Valve GDC 2009](https://steamcdn-a.akamaihd.net/apps/valve/2009/ai_systems_of_l4d_mike_booth.pdf)). Those timings were tuned for campaign chapters of roughly 10–20 minutes, the same order as a Last Lightkeepers run.

Alien: Isolation does the same thing with a different input. Its **menace gauge** rises on proximity, line of sight, and whether the Alien could *reach* the player quickly — threat state, not damage taken — and **at peak menace the director pulls the Alien away**, into neighbouring areas or into the vents, chosen near where the player is likely to go next. The write-up states the reason flatly: the player "can't be scared all the time, or they'll give up" ([The Perfect Organism, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-perfect-organism-the-ai-of-alien-isolation)). Resident Evil 4 (2005) went further and rubber-banded *down*, reducing enemy count and aggression after repeated deaths ([Prima Games](https://primagames.com/tips/how-to-change-difficulty-in-resident-evil-4-remake-difficulty-levels-guide)). **Not one documented system escalates in response to high player stress.** Across all the failure cases surfaced in this research, every documented failure of sustained horror is a *pacing* failure (too constant, too long, too scripted) or a *social* failure (voice chat reframing the fiction). None is a failure of insufficient threat power.

The reconciliation is available and it does not require touching the locked rule. **Separate what the meter governs from what the meter emits.** Risk of Rain 2 is the shipped precedent for the monotonic half: a time-driven difficulty scalar with a persistent HUD bar, where the player's chosen difficulty is implemented as a **rate multiplier on the same curve** — Drizzle at 50% speed, Monsoon at 150% ([Risk of Rain 2 Wiki](https://riskofrain2.wiki.gg/wiki/Difficulty)). GTFO is the precedent for making monotonicity legible rather than cruel: its **error alarms "often can't be shut off until the expedition ends"**, and the important property is not the ramp but that players *know* it cannot be turned off, which converts the ramp from a problem to be solved into a clock to be beaten ([GTFO Wiki](https://gtfo.wiki.gg/wiki/Alarm)). So Storm Charge rises monotonically and governs the building's *authority* — which procedures are available, how many machines are awake, which routes are locked, how much of the map is denied. Underneath it, a Director decides *when* that authority is spent, with Valve's Peak Fade guard so a trough never begins mid-engagement, and Alien's withdrawal behaviour so the apex pulls off a cornered crew rather than converging on them. The meter never falls; the pressure still breathes. Nothing about "only ever rises" requires that every second be louder than the last.

Two cautions on the ramp's shape. For a 10–12 minute run, **the live risk is saturation**: a meter that reaches its loudest state at minute six has no remaining vocabulary for minutes seven through twelve, so the most distinct changes must be reserved for the final third and the audio must start sparse specifically to leave headroom. And the escalation must be *specific*, not merely louder. The cautionary analogue is the US colour-coded terror advisory, criticised for "vague and overly broad alerts," with a DHS official conceding that "elevating the level across the board is a fairly blunt instrument" ([Stateline](https://stateline.org/2004/03/09/state-officials-push-localized-color-coded-alerts/)). A player should be able to name what the last step added — *it hunts by sound now; the service corridors are sealed now* — not just notice that the room got darker. One experimental finding supports continuous ramps over discrete stings: a two-second warning beep "distracted people from their main task and pushed them into rushed decisions," and the researchers replaced it with a background sound that grew louder as the deadline approached ([Research Catalogue](https://www.researchcatalogue.net/view/513707/513719)). Reserve discrete stings for the small number of irreversible threshold crossings, and make each one mean exactly one thing.

## Fear habituates before players notice, so changing the demand beats changing the volume

The most actionable empirical result in the fear literature is a dissociation. Grizzard et al. (2015) found repeated play produced habituation in **both** biophysiological and self-reported arousal, with **stronger evidence of generalization in the biophysiological data** — the dampening carried over to *novel* game content ([Communication Monographs](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03637751.2014.971418)). Two consequences follow directly. First, **by run twenty a veteran's body has likely stopped responding while they still report the game as tense**, which means playtest self-report will overestimate surviving dread and design decisions driven by it will be wrong in a predictable direction. Second, because generalization was stronger physiologically, **adding new monsters and maps of the same kind should be expected to deliver less re-sensitisation than designers assume.** Changing the *skin* is weak; changing the *kind of demand* is better supported.

No published decay curve exists — the one study designed to produce sessions-to-habituation numbers for horror games lost its physiological data to large-scale data loss ([ScienceDirect, 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1875952122000623)) — and **no study measures arousal in co-op horror across sessions at all.** Treat every decay-rate number as absent.

What survives learning is not surprise but uncertainty, and the distinction has theoretical teeth. Temporal uncertainty about threat raises anxiety through anticipation and hazard rate, independent of the threat's magnitude ([PMC, 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11192096/)). The paradox of suspense — people feel suspense on re-reading, when the outcome is known — is the theoretical reason stakes-based tension outlives rule-learning. Alien's operationalisation is the craft answer: **deliberately degraded competence** rather than increased lethality. The Alien visits areas of interest in a suboptimal order, backtracks as if doubtful, distinguishes "search" from "spot" locations, hears footsteps and detects the motion tracker at roughly 1.5 metres, and runs short rear ray-traces so creeping up directly behind it fails. Its power is fixed — one-hit lethal from the start. What varies is which behaviours are *available* ([The Perfect Organism](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-perfect-organism-the-ai-of-alien-isolation)). For an unkillable apex, power escalation is barely available anyway, so the whole budget has to go on behavioural variance and pacing frequency.

Now the hazard that applies specifically to this project and that no amount of good AI fixes. **Four players on voice chat convert horror into comedy, and this is the best-attested failure mode in the co-op record.** Lethal Company is routinely described as horror solo and comedy in a group, with **proximity voice chat named as the proximate cause** ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/lethal-companys-co-op-horror-comedy-is-like-phasmophobia-but-immediately-more-chaotic-and-deadly/); [TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/lethal-company-workplace-horror-comedy-politics-social-impact-quota/)). The apparent counter-case resolves in a way that matters: a HICSS 2024 analysis of The Dark Pictures Anthology's shared-story mode found co-op *amplified* discomfort, because players felt it for themselves and their partner ([HICSS](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/3f16df70-ddd8-47d8-8b58-a7698eee2d62/full)) — but that is two players, turn-based, with permanent character death. **The variable is not co-op per se but whether the group's attention is on the threat or on each other, which tracks stake severity and voice-channel load.** Non-lethal knockdown-and-containment sits on the Lethal Company side by default. Comedy drift is the baseline outcome unless failure is given a stake players feel *for each other*.

Two mechanisms earn their place against this. Phasmophobia made the voice channel itself a threat surface: hunt search behaviour keys off **microphone loudness**, so players whisper to each other or be located ([DualShockers](https://www.dualshockers.com/phasmophobia-ghosts-listen-for-players-when-hunting/)). That is the single most transferable idea found for the four-people-talking problem, and it is directly compatible with machines executing degraded instructions — a Glimmer that logs noise is a loss-prevention device, not a malicious one. The second is register. The criticism on institutional horror converges on the finding that flat, categorising *language* is the horror device: SCP-style containment procedures "derive their authority from their use of language," clinical and amoral in tone ([SFRA Review 51.1](https://sfrareview.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/sfra-review-51.1-secured-contained.pdf)); a 2025 SUNY thesis locates terror in "the cold efficiency of institutions that reward obedience over empathy" ([SUNY SOAR](https://soar.suny.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/a7366cf9-ccb6-41ad-b9ed-588bae60f130/content)). The containment *notice* — the shelf tag, the log line, the spoken procedure — does the work, and because it is text and image as much as audio, it is **comparatively voice-chat-robust**. That is a design asset worth building deliberately: the one horror channel four shouting players cannot drown.

## Indifference has machine precedents, no full-length one, and a known failure into inertness

The locked thesis is well-chosen but the precedent base is narrower than it looks. The strongest documented cases of a non-malicious threat that still frightens are *mechanical* (Alien: Isolation's Working Joes, SOMA's machines running on damaged purpose) and *institutional* (the Severance/Kafka lineage). **Natural** indifference has the weakest fear record and the loudest backlash — which suggests nature reads as "obstacle" faster than machines do. Area X and the Zone, often cited for this, are documented by their author as *ambiguous* rather than indifferent; Publishers Weekly describes Area X as "fearsome yet largely unconcerned with the humans who enter it" ([Publishers Weekly](https://publishersweekly.com/pw/print/20240819/95750-jeff-vandermeer-journeys-into-the-unknown.html)), and VanderMeer has called it both threatening and in some respects benign. Useful as tone, not as proof.

The Working Joes are the closest thing to a direct ancestor, and the critical account of why they work maps almost exactly onto Chris's design: calm bureaucratic speech ("You do not have an appointment," "Running causes accidents") delivered in a dull tone, **indifference replacing rage**, relentlessness at a walk rather than a sprint, and a deliberately uncanny body. The same editorial makes the contrast that matters here: with the Xenomorph "death is quick and almost painless," while the Joes approach at a walk, take their time, and produce "a sense of inevitability" ([Dread Central](https://www.dreadcentral.com/editorials/491659/alien-isolation-the-relentless-terror-of-the-working-joes/)). **Slow-and-inevitable is the register critics already identify as the scarier of the two, and it is exactly the register of being walked toward and held.** Two honest caveats: there is no retrievable Creative Assembly statement on *why* the Joes speak politely — everything about their effect is critical interpretation, not developer intent — and in Isolation **indifference was never asked to carry a whole game alone.** The Joes were a secondary threat. A project where indifference is the sole thesis has no direct precedent at full length.

The failure mode is documented twice, from both directions. **SOMA's ambiguity experiment failed in playtesting.** Frictional wanted players unsure whether monsters were hostile, so they would approach cautiously and watch; instead players either "ran straight up to the monsters" or ignored them. The fix was to **abandon ambiguity rather than refine it**, polarising monsters into "either really docile or pretty aggressive," with Thomas Grip later saying they should have been *clearer*, with "more foreboding behaviour so that players knew what to expect" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-tricky-design-problem-of-i-soma-i-s-memorable-monsters)). Four players will run the same test in the first thirty seconds, four times faster. And the live 2026 case is Subnautica 2, where **the studio itself concedes predator encounters "can feel frustrating rather than tense,"** attributing it mainly to *unreliable avoidance tools*, with a developer "stunned by players with no sense of danger — 'I don't think we thought people would build by wolves'" ([Digital Citizen](https://www.digitalcitizen.life/subnautica-2-will-not-add-lethal-combat-but-predator-encounters-are-getting-clearer-survival-tools/); [GamesRadar via inkl](https://www.inkl.com/news/subnautica-2-creature-mitigation-a-priority-for-updates-as-dev-is-stunned-by-players-with-no-sense-of-danger-i-dont-think-we-thought-people-would-build-by-wolves)). **"No negotiation" needs a substitute lever — deterrence, delay, misdirection — or dread converts into annoyance.** And the threshold that triggers hostility must be crossable *by accident*: Steam threads report the Joes as "numb unless you walk directly in front of one," meaning a chunk of players read them as inert furniture. **Indifference degrades into inertness if the detection threshold is too tight.**

Grip's formula is the one to hold onto: **"strict but fuzzy rules"** — legible enough that players build a mental model, loose enough that the model never closes. He wanted players to *over-attribute* intelligence. The defensible position for this project is that indifference is not a substitute for intent, it is **a vacancy the player fills.** Keep the machine's *purpose* legible; put the mystery in the schedule and the thresholds, not in whether the thing is hostile.

Containment-as-failure is the project's most original ground and its least validated. **No source in this research analyses a threat whose action is to detain rather than kill, and no game precedent exists where non-lethal capture is the primary failure state.** Structurally it is Kafka's *Castle* — awaiting an authority that never resolves — more than it is any horror game. That is a strength for originality and a risk for validation. The locked rules do, however, sit on the strong side of the one pattern that demonstrably gives non-lethal failure teeth: the loss must be accumulated by effort in this session, visible and countable before it is lost, and not automatically recoverable. Lethal Company satisfies all three, and **a crew wipe losing the entire haul is structurally identical to its "no living player aboard at departure" rule — the single mechanic players report as genuinely painful** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lethal_Company); [Steam discussions](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1966720/discussions/0/4032472470670005882)). Individual death there, by contrast, is priced at a **"Player Body," value 5**, and the community built mods to *add* a death penalty ([Lethal Company Wiki](https://lethalcompany.wiki.gg/wiki/Scrap); [Configurable Quota](https://thunderstore.io/c/lethal-company/p/seechela/Configurable_Quota/)). The lesson is blunt: **the failure state must be priced in the same unit as the objective.** Haul in, haul lost — the locked rules already do this.

And the downed-player-keeps-loot rule is probably this project's strongest single lever, precisely *because* there is no bleed-out timer. A contained crewmate is simultaneously a person and a quantity of endowed haul, so abandoning them costs twice in the same decision, and the indefinite wait makes that decision **deliberative rather than panicked** — computable, arguable over voice, self-blaming afterward. One structural requirement follows: **incremental banking is what makes an all-or-nothing wipe rule feel fair.** If there is no way to ferry part of a haul out mid-run, the wipe reads as punitive rather than as a gamble the crew took, and the "we stayed too long" self-blame that the whole design depends on never fires.

## Testing the four night states and five pillars against the record

**The four night states are the best-supported idea in the proposal**, because they are a near-exact match for the one documented anti-habituation technique in the record. Alien: Isolation's behaviour tree runs **over 100 nodes with roughly 30 at the top level selecting behaviour type**, and parts of it **start locked and unlock as the player meets conditions**, so the threat appears to learn and new threats keep appearing. Shipped builds also unlock behaviours at campaign checkpoints so the alien keeps pace with improving players ([The Perfect Organism](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-perfect-organism-the-ai-of-alien-isolation)). Routine → Containment → Emergency → Lockdown *is* a staged instruction-set unlock, dressed in fiction that explains it. "The machine's task list changed" is a far cheaper and more legible escalation than "the machine got stronger," and it is the version that keeps the player's mental model valid but incomplete. The Sweeper that first herds and later drives you into machinery, the Ficus that first holds and later pins you where your lantern makes you a beacon, the Glimmer whose scan summons the correct staff rather than dealing damage — these are instruction-set changes on existing systems, which is the precise definition of a cheap modifier rather than new content.

One rule must come with it, and it is non-negotiable on the evidence. Per Creative Assembly's Andy Bray, **no unlock conditions are ever driven by player deaths**, because that would punish players through hidden triggers they cannot diagnose. For a game whose failure state is knockdown and containment, this means **Storm Charge must never rise from knockdowns or containments as such.** It may rise from theft, light, depth, alarms, elapsed time and the extraction call — things the crew chose — but the moment a downed teammate accelerates the night, the escalation reads as the game kicking you while you're down, and Chris's own fifth pillar (never secretly rubber-band) is violated by his own escalation trigger. This is the single most important implementation constraint in the report.

**Where the framework collides with the evidence:** the Lockdown state as described — "exits become a hard holdout" — is the one rung that runs against the pacing literature rather than with it. A hard holdout at maximum meter is sustained maximum threat, which Valve names as a failure mode in so many words ("constant, unchanging combat is fatiguing") and which Alien's director actively prevents by forcing the threat backstage. GTFO is the shipped cautionary case: its forced alarms drew sustained criticism as a mechanic players **resented rather than feared**, and the diagnosis is that the pressure arrives *without a choice attached* ([Steam Community](https://steamcommunity.com/app/493520/discussions/4/3344417177389251034)). **An escalating meter is only dread if the player holds a lever.** If there is ever a stretch where leaving is impossible, the Storm Charge in that stretch is noise. The fix is not to soften Lockdown but to ensure it still contains a decision — more than one exit, a cost to each, the option to abandon part of the haul to buy a route — and to put a Peak Fade guard in front of it so the Warden's arrival lands on a crew that just got a trough, not on one that has been pinned for ninety seconds.

The Routine state carries the opposite risk. **"Nothing happens" dread measured roughly 50/50 even among solo Frictional testers**, with some excited and others finding hiding "getting old" — and four people on voice chat will fill that silence with jokes. Routine needs to be short, and it needs to give the crew work to do (valuing loot by lamplight, deciding routes) rather than asking them to be afraid of an empty aisle.

Of the five replayability pillars, **four are well-supported and one is a liability.** *Mastery over enemy health scaling* is correct and cheap: Hopoo built Risk of Rain 2's eight-rung Eclipse ladder as "**an experimental weekend project**," on a game that retained 11.63% of peak ([Hopoo Development Thoughts #16](https://devtrackers.gg/risk-of-rain/p/3a6c1b24-hopoo-games-development-thoughts-16)). *Run variation via a few authored events, altered routes, active departments and enemy combinations* is exactly right, and the evidence says why: **procedural generation of space is a weak retention tool; procedural generation of situation is a strong one.** Blue Manchu's Jonathan Chey *baked* Void Bastards' level layouts to disk because "logical layouts with patterns players could learn made the most sense," and moved variance into module *state* ([Game World Observer](https://gameworldobserver.com/2019/08/06/void-bastards)); Ghost Ship describes its generation as "a mix between hand-crafted components and code" with designers controlling the variation ([GamingBolt](https://gamingbolt.com/deep-rock-galactic-interview-heigh-ho-off-to-plunder-we-go/)). Nobody in this dataset gets retention from terrain novelty. *Co-op crises where one player is restrained and another must choose between rescue, loot, or extraction* is the pillar that converts the locked containment rule into the game's signature decision, and it is the strongest asset in the whole design. *Fair escalation* is supported by Bray's no-death-triggered-unlocks rule and by the Lethal Company pattern where loss traces to one identifiable greedy choice, converting punishment into self-blame.

**Pillar three — visible long-term change: restored rooms, rescued keepers, opened districts, relic history — is the one to cut back hard.** It is the most emotionally appealing item on the list and the most dangerous for a solo developer, because it is horizontal content wearing a progression costume. Søren Lundgaard's warning is the most useful quote in this research for a one-person team: "**As you build on top of an existing game, adding system after system, you risk making everything more complicated,**" and "it also complicates things in development as you have to make sure you are not breaking things for past seasons" ([Screen Rant](https://screenrant.com/deep-rock-galactic-interview/)). That is a 35-person studio finding backward compatibility burdensome. The real cost of horizontal content is not authoring the next piece; it is **the permanent compatibility liability of everything already shipped.** GTFO is the consumption counter-argument: a year into Early Access, **under 1% of players had completed Rundown 1 and 91.18% of all expeditions failed**, from a studio that said expeditions remained "a lot of handcrafting" even with purpose-built tools and that "we don't want to grow the team" ([DualShockers, stats](https://www.dualshockers.com/gtfo-statistics-2020-show-how-hard-rundowns-are-to-complete/); [DualShockers, interview](https://www.dualshockers.com/gtfo-early-access-2019-interview/)). Hand-authored content has the worst ratio in the dataset. Keep a *small, fixed* set of visible world changes — the ones that are state flags on existing rooms, authored once — and resist every instinct to extend the set after launch.

Chris's framing statement — "Early on, you survive the store. Later, you decide how much of the night you are willing to wake up in order to bring something back" — is the most accurate single sentence about what the design is. It describes a **voluntary difficulty economy**, which is precisely the structure Hades used: **Heat gates rewards**, so the player raising the difficulty is simultaneously the player unlocking things ([Hades Wiki](https://hades.fandom.com/wiki/Heat)). That coupling is what makes a ladder self-motivating with no content added. It is also, worth noting, in tension with the locked monotonic meter: if the night escalates on its own schedule regardless, the player is not choosing how much to wake up — the clock is. **Making that sentence true requires that a meaningful share of the Storm Charge come from crew decisions (light, theft, depth, alarms) rather than from elapsed time alone.** That is both a design requirement and a readability requirement, and it is the thing that makes Chris's own fifth pillar deliverable.

## The endgame one developer can run is a ladder of withheld information, not a treadmill

The cost evidence here is unusually clear, and the gap is roughly two orders of magnitude: **one weekend (Eclipse, stated) versus "a lot of handcrafting" by a studio that refused to grow and then declared an ending (GTFO rundowns, stated).** A ladder's rungs are reorderings of content the game already has, so rung N+1 costs tuning while content item N+1 costs authoring. For a developer whose marginal budget is near zero, that is the only structure that scales down to one person.

But the conventional ladder — Hazard 5, Eclipse 8, Nightmare, Heat 32 — is almost entirely implemented as "enemies hit harder and you die faster," and that implementation is **ruled out by the locked rules.** The locked rules force a different ladder currency, and three candidates have precedent. **Tempo**: the meter rises faster, knockdowns cost more meter, rescues take longer (precedent: Phasmophobia's sanity drain speed and hunt duration). **Capacity**: fewer simultaneous knockdowns the crew can absorb, fewer rescues, less equipment, heavier haul, extraction further away (precedent: Phasmophobia's hiding-place count; DRG's **Point Extraction scaler running 0.70 at Hazard 1 to 1.10 at Hazard 5**, which raises the *resource requirement* rather than the threat ([DRG Wiki](https://deeprockgalactic.wiki.gg/wiki/Difficulty_Scaling))). **Legibility**: less information — Nightmare hides one evidence type, damaged monitoring equipment, fuse box starting off ([Kinetic Games](https://kineticgames.co.uk/news/phasmophobia-nightmare)). **Legibility is the cheapest of the three, because removing information requires no new systems at all, and it is the one that most directly serves escalating dread.** A ladder built on "you lose tempo and sight faster" rather than "you die faster" is the only version compatible with the locked rules, and Phasmophobia demonstrates it works.

Phasmophobia's Custom Difficulty is the template to copy nearly wholesale: roughly **30 orthogonal parameters** across player, threat and environment axes, shipped in a single update (v0.7.0.0, 2022-09-27), with **each option tied to a reward multiplier** and three saved presets ([Kinetic Games, Apocalypse](https://kineticgames.co.uk/news/phasmophobia-apocalypse)). Every one of those options is a numeric knob on an already-shipped system; **a modifier that needs new art, new AI behaviour or new audio is not a modifier, it is content with a checkbox.** The ongoing cost is not authoring them — it is policing the reward economy against degenerate combinations, which Kinetic did twice (an 80% reduction to Custom multipliers, then 0x for disabling item loss). That cost is bounded and schedulable rather than continuous, which is the right shape for one person. Supergiant reworking the Pact of Punishment twice and relabelling it "2nd Edition" is the warning on the other side: **a modular condition system has a hidden tuning cost in combinatorics, because every condition must be sane in combination with every other.** A small number of orthogonal conditions is dramatically cheaper than a large number of interacting ones. Spend restraint here.

Weekly challenges work without a live operation, and the minimum viable version is documented. Slay the Spire's Daily Climb shipped as **3 modifiers drawn from a fixed pool of 28, selected by a seed shared by everyone, with no leaderboard at all** ("leaderboards will eventually be added as well"), and when the leaderboard arrived it ran on **Steam's own service, not a Mega Crit server** ([Weekly Patch 14](https://slaythespire.wiki.gg/wiki/Weekly_Patch_14_-_Bullet_Time); [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/au/slay-the-spire-is-out-now-with-more-pc-specific-features-coming)). Derive the seed client-side from the UTC week number, draw N modifiers from the pool that already exists, ship with no leaderboard, and if one is ever added default it to friends-only — which is the correct choice for a private-lobby game and sidesteps the fact that Spelunky's, Isaac's and Slay the Spire's leaderboards were all compromised or cheat-flagged. Set expectations accordingly: **DRG's "complete a Deep Dive" achievement sits at 18.2% against 16.9% for "complete 100 missions"** ([Steam global stats](https://steamcommunity.com/stats/548430/achievements/)). Weekly content tracks the already-committed cohort; it is a retention mechanism for people who already play a lot, not a reactivation mechanism. Worth having at zero marginal cost, not worth expecting a curve lift from.

The highest-leverage finding in the endgame research is about who authors the endgame. **Barotrauma retains 28.14% of its all-time peak — the best in the dataset — and has 84,126 Workshop entries of which 47,954, or 57%, are submarines: the one content type with a dedicated in-game editor** ([Steam Workshop](https://steamcommunity.com/workshop/browse/?appid=602960&browsesort=trend&section=readytouseitems)). High-volume UGC follows the editor you ship. For a 10–12 minute co-op run, the analogous editor is **a challenge/modifier preset editor with shareable preset codes** — Phasmophobia's Custom Difficulty plus a share string. That is player-authored endgame at near-zero authoring cost and, crucially, **zero compatibility liability**, because a preset is just a parameter set the game already validates. Everything else about modding should follow Zeekerss's cheap path rather than DRG's expensive one: do not publish an API, do not host, keep a `previous` Steam branch so modded installs survive patches, flag modded clients in the lobby browser, and occasionally ship inert hooks to unbreak what you broke. Note also what players will ask for: **MoreCompany, which removes the four-player lobby cap, is the single most-downloaded Lethal Company mod at 27 million** ([Thunderstore](https://thunderstore.io/c/lethal-company/)). With 1–4 players locked, that demand will arrive and a mod will meet it. Decide in advance whether higher counts are a supported ladder rung or a thing mods do.

Finally, the one piece of advice every small team in this research converged on independently: **declare an ending.** RyseUp after a decade — "the initial experience was designed to be a 25/50 hours gameplay experience, there's no way we can continue to provide content for players with over 1000 hours of gameplay" ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/games/fps/after-a-decade-of-development-roboquests-developer-is-calling-time-on-its-roguelite-shooter-theres-no-way-we-can-continue-to-provide-content-for-players-with-over-1-000-hours-of-gameplay/)). Supergiant at 1.0. Motion Twin by handing off. 10 Chambers at Rundown 8. **Lethal Company's permanent Early Access is the counter-case and the one that generated the most player complaint.** Planning the end state at ship time and saying so publicly converts "abandoned" into "complete." The one thing to keep doing after content stops is communication — Chey's retrospective on Wild Bastards is the whole argument: "With Wild Bastards I think we just lost contact… I think that was really bad" ([PC Gamer via syndication](https://tech.yahoo.com/articles/immersive-sim-sickos-rejoice-makers-121528504.html)).

## Private-only co-op makes the group the churn unit, and there is no repair path

This risk deserves its own heading because it is structural, it follows from a locked rule, and nothing in the design mitigates it.

If social ties are the driver that carries hours twenty-plus — and the Fairyland and PLOS ONE results are the best evidence available that they are — then **the churn unit in a private-lobby game is the group, not the player.** Garcia et al.'s formal account of Friendster's collapse describes the cascade: users leave when their cost/benefit tips, and each departure lowers the benefit for their neighbours ([arXiv:1302.6109](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.6109)). A game with no public matchmaking has no mechanism to repair a group whose members drift. That **amplifies decay rather than damping it.** The consolation prize is real but limited: PvE-only private lobbies mean no anti-cheat upkeep, which was the second cited cause of Arc Raiders' 2026 collapse and is a genuine structural advantage for a solo developer.

The levers that follow are cheap and one-off, and they should be treated as retention features rather than conveniences. Low session cost is the big one — **10–12 minutes fits a friend group's scheduling far better than a 40-minute raid**, and this is one place the locked design is unambiguously correct. Beyond that: graceful tolerance for partial groups so a three-person night is a real night rather than a degraded one, frictionless invite and rejoin, drop-in continuity, and **asymmetric roles that make a specific friend's presence feel irreplaceable** (the portable half of DRG's class design, and the part that costs nothing recurring).

One caution the evidence insists on. "Social sustains retention longest" does not mean the game *earns* that retention. If the group is the retention mechanism, **the game is substitutable within the group** — which is exactly the observed pattern of friend groups migrating en masse from Lethal Company to the next cheap co-op horror title. Social design buys you the right to be the game the group plays this month. Mastery depth is what makes you the game they come back to, and it is the only lever on that list a solo developer can keep deepening at zero marginal cost.

## Conclusion

The proposed next slice is the right one, and the research sharpens its success criterion. Building **one enemy that starts as containment, visibly changes behaviour after a Storm threshold, and forces a real escape decision** is the correct test, because it is simultaneously a test of the only cheap escalation mechanism in the record (instruction-set unlock rather than stat scaling), the only failure state the project has no precedent for (containment with a price tag on it), and the one thing the whole design lives or dies on (whether four people on voice chat take a non-lethal threat seriously). Build it with a Peak Fade guard and a withdrawal behaviour from day one, so the slice tests a paced escalation rather than a sustained one. And judge it on behaviour, not on report: **Grizzard's dissociation finding means the crew will tell you it was tense long after it stopped being so, so measure whether they left loot behind, whether they argued about the rescue, and whether anyone said "we should go" out loud.** Those are observable. "It felt scary" is not.

Two things to hold in mind while building it. First, the retention question may be the wrong question to optimise. Lethal Company retains 2.29% of its peak and sold ten million copies; the structures that produce a high retention percentage in this dataset all cost a content pipeline that one developer plus AI agents cannot run. **The honest plan is a steep decay curve by design, a ladder of withheld information and tempo to give the committed minority somewhere to climb, a preset editor so players author the long tail, and a publicly declared ending that makes the decay look like completion rather than abandonment.** Second, the most original thing in this design is also the least evidenced. There is no shipped game in which being detained rather than killed is the primary failure state, which means Chris is not iterating on a known quantity — he is running the experiment. The two cautionary cases worth rereading before the slice ships are SOMA, where ambiguity about hostility failed in playtesting and the fix was to be *clearer*, and Subnautica 2, where a non-lethal threat without reliable avoidance tools produced frustration rather than tension and players built their houses next to the predators. **Both failures were failures of legibility, not of ferocity. The machine should be perfectly readable; only the night's schedule should be unknown.**
