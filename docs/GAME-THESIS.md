# Last Lightkeepers — Game Thesis

## The claim

*Last Lightkeepers* is a first-person, one-to-four-player co-op recovery game about making a damaged world hold together one night at a time.

Players enter places the storm has made dangerous, recover what the living need, and decide how much danger they will accept before the night closes. They do not save the world by killing its monsters. They make it possible for people to live through another week: a pump runs, a route opens, a record survives, a hall gets warmer, and eventually a city can remember itself.

The emotional promise is not power fantasy. It is: **"We got something home."**

## What the game is — and is not

It is a tense, readable co-op expedition where light is simultaneously a tool, a beacon, and a commitment. It is built for friends who want stories of narrow escapes, imperfect plans, and useful things brought back.

It is not a loot-shooter, a permanent-survival sandbox, an inventory spreadsheet, or a sequence of disposable haunted-house maps. Combat creates time and space; it cannot erase the night. Progression expands what the crew can attempt; it must not turn the game into an effortless farm.

## The world model

Each raid is one night. The storm is arriving whether the crew acts or not. Its charge wakes systems whose last instructions have degraded into rigid behavior.

The machines are not secretly evil and do not need a villainous master. They are dangerous because they continue to do a job after the conditions that made the job humane are gone:

| Place | Last job, gone wrong |
| --- | --- |
| Department store | Stock, clean, contain loss, preserve property |
| Hospital | Triage, isolate, transport, preserve life by protocol |
| Train station | Route, hold, evacuate, keep platforms clear |
| Downtown | Maintain utilities, protect infrastructure, respond to flood |
| Library / museum | Preserve records and objects against loss |

That rule gives every enemy and threat a readable purpose. The crew is not being punished arbitrarily; it is entering a system at night, carrying property out of it, and interfering with its work.

## The playable argument

Every core system must strengthen one question:

> What are we willing to risk tonight so someone can live better tomorrow?

The answer is played, not explained.

1. The crew leaves Lanternhold with a small, comprehensible recovery job.
2. A lantern reveals routes, loot, and threats, while adding charge to the machines.
3. The store asks for a choice: take the safe, useful thing home, or push deeper for something that changes what the community can do.
4. The crew escapes before the storm closes the building, or loses the trip.
5. The recovery visibly changes Lanternhold and opens the next kind of job.

Extraction must be a climax rather than a door: it is called, announced, converged upon, and held. A loss must hurt without making a player feel their evening was erased. The durable reward is capability and visible restoration; the disposable reward is the haul from this night.

## The first game: one department store

The first vertical slice is a dense, authored department store, not a tiny version of an entire mall. Its purpose is to teach the whole game through space.

- The entrance makes a single objective and a safe return route legible.
- Sales floor and fitting rooms teach sightlines, cover, mannequin threat, and selective light.
- Stockrooms and loading areas teach route choice, sound, and valuable supplies.
- A flooded basement teaches that time and water are part of the same night; a pump buys time but never deletes danger.
- The upper overlook teaches exposure and storm lightning: the building is briefly revealed, then altered by what the flash woke.

The first successful run should take roughly ten to twelve minutes. It must contain one useful recovery, one intelligible threat, one voluntary risk, and one extraction story worth retelling.

## Progression: capability with cost

The player is not collecting numbers for their own sake. Every meaningful recovery answers an operational question at Lanternhold or in the next raid: what can we carry, repair, reach, understand, or protect now?

Progression has three visible layers:

1. **Survival capability** — tools and routes that create a small amount of time or space, never permanent immunity.
2. **Restoration** — physical changes at Lanternhold that show the community becoming more habitable.
3. **Civic recovery** — later zones restore the knowledge and infrastructure required to hold more than one building together.

No upgrade may remove the lantern decision, shorten risk out of existence, or sell combat/movement advantage that breaks co-op tuning. If a reward makes a future danger trivial, it is not progression; it is debt against the game.

## Co-op and the people worth returning for

Friends can always play together. Co-op is not a reward to unlock.

Rescued Keepers are progression, but they should not become noisy escort missions or automated lantern carriers. Humans own every lantern. Early Keepers add a route marker, radio warning, repair task, or specific bit of learned knowledge from Lanternhold; only much later should a field Keeper become a carefully authored companion. Their help must always have a cost, signal, or protection requirement that preserves the central light-and-risk rule.

## The end

The long game ends in civic recovery, not victory over weather. A reclaimed library and museum turn Lanternhold's small act of keeping records into a city-scale act of keeping a people alive. The crew learns how to build flood defenses, restore routes, and preserve what the storm would erase.

The storm still comes. The world is not made safe. The ending says that safety was never the promise: **things can be built to hold.**

This supports continued play after the narrative ending. New nights, damaged places, and restoration work remain meaningful without pretending the player failed to finish the game.

## Red-team findings

These are the ways the project fails if they are not actively guarded against.

| Failure | Why it kills the game | Guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Darkness is merely inconvenience | Players turn lights off and wait | Darkness must hide routes, isolate the crew, and leave the storm advancing anyway |
| Light is only punishment | The signature mechanic feels like a tax | Light must reveal value, safety, and decisive information before it creates trouble |
| Full-loss runs feel arbitrary | Friends stop after one bad wipe | Keep runs short, recovery goals clear, knockdown recoverable, and lasting progress visible |
| Enemies are generic chase props | The world loses its premise | Every threat must express a location-specific last job and telegraph through at least two channels |
| Audio is required to survive | Voice chat and accessibility exclude players | Pair sound with visible behavior, readable light, captions, and intensity controls |
| NPC Keepers solve problems for players | The lantern rule and co-op ownership disappear | Keepers support decisions; human players make and pay for them |
| The department store becomes a giant empty map | Scale replaces density and fear | Build a dense, furnished, legible first floor before expanding the footprint |
| Fidelity arrives before a playable loop | Expensive art hides an unproven game | No production art push before a repeatable greybox run produces voluntary replays |
| The fiction explains itself too early | Discovery becomes a tooltip | Let spaces and behavior imply the last-job rule; let earned characters and places deepen it later |

## Evidence, not optimism

The vertical slice earns expansion only when external players can do the following without a narrated tutorial:

- understand the first job and leave the store;
- explain why they used or avoided light;
- recognize a threat before it hits them through two readable channels;
- argue about whether to push deeper or extract;
- complete or lose a run and immediately want one more;
- describe a visible, useful change at Lanternhold caused by what they recovered.

If those tests fail, more zones, more monsters, better materials, and more lore are not the answer. The answer is repairing the core decision until the player can feel it.

## Production position

The project is now a Steam, PC-first Unreal Engine game. The Roblox prototype is a frozen reference for what was learned, not a second product. The next production proof is not a mall and not a trailer: it is one networked, first-person department-store expedition that works in a private friend lobby, with a server-authoritative lantern, a readable threat, a real extraction hold, and a homeward recovery loop.

The thesis in one sentence:

> *Last Lightkeepers* is a first-person co-op recovery game in which every light you make, every machine you wake, and every object you carry home asks the same question: what are you willing to risk tonight so someone can live better tomorrow?
