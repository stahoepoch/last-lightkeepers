# The four anchors, and the four ways of knowing where you are

`DECISIONS.md` 19 names four anchor stores with no real trademarks in them:
**Boswell's, Sayers, Penhurst, Bellevue.** Chris is assigning them identities,
and they are landing — probably not by accident — on four genuinely different
kinds of spatial knowledge.

That is the structure worth locking, because it makes the second store a new
game rather than a new skin. See `KNOWLEDGE-SPACE-TIME.md` for why these three
categories are the right axis.

| Store | Real-world shape | Knowledge it gives | What a crew learns to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Boswell's** | The old local one | **Survey** | Read the plan off the mirrored ceiling; take shortcuts |
| **Sayers** | Sears, with automotive | **Route** | Learn sequences and follow sound; never see the shape |
| **Penhurst** | JCPenney, crooked | **Landmark** | Navigate by named objects; peek around oblique corners |
| **Bellevue** | *unassigned* | **Vertical survey** (proposed) | See three floors at once; think in section, not plan |

---

## Sayers — automotive, utilitarian, tight

Chris: *an automotive repair area like Sears, interior rather utilitarian and
tight.*

**Knowledge type: route.** Tight and utilitarian means short sightlines and no
vantage anywhere. You cannot form a plan view of this building, so you learn
it as a sequence of turns. It is the store where you say "left at the parts
counter, then the second bay."

**The signature affordance: the machines are still running.** An automotive
centre has compressors, lifts and air lines — plant that cycles and hums.
`THE-STORE.md` already gives the basement a directional spine of boiler and
switchgear hum, *"so a player navigating by sound has one here even with the
lantern lit and every sightline black."* Sayers makes that the character of
the whole store rather than one floor.

That is the route-knowledge instrument: sound gives you sequence and
direction, never shape.

**What the automotive area changes structurally:**

- **Roll-up bay doors to the outside.** Sayers is the only anchor with a
  direct exterior connection on the shop floor. That reshapes extraction: not
  a loading dock you hold, but a door you have to **raise**, loudly, with the
  storm on the other side.
- **Semi-exterior light.** Storm light comes through the bay openings, which
  locally inverts the lantern's value — the one place in the store where you
  can see without paying, and are therefore seen.
- **Service pits and lifts.** Vertical hazard in a single-storey space. A pit
  is a hole you can fall in, hide in, or lose someone in.
- **Tire racks.** Tall, dense, continuous — the best sightline blockers in the
  game, and they come in rows.
- **The parts counter and its back room of numbered bins.** A relic-dense
  space with an organising logic: things are *filed*, so finding a specific
  one is a reading task, which is a lantern task.

**And it is the doorway-effect store.** Tight utilitarian layouts are made of
small rooms with doors, which is the condition that produces the most
boundary-crossing forgetting. Sayers is where the Fieldboard matters most —
players will genuinely not remember what they saw two rooms back.

## Penhurst — crooked, two ways out, angles, a shop inside the shop

Chris: *very crooked, two entrances/exits, lots of angles, an interior
Sephora-like store, a hair salon.*

**Knowledge type: landmark.** This is the important consequence of crooked
geometry and it is worth stating plainly: **you cannot build a mental grid of
a non-orthogonal building.** Survey knowledge is structurally unavailable, and
the angles keep forking routes, so route knowledge is unreliable too. What is
left is landmarks — and Chris has already specified two excellent ones.

**The signature affordance: oblique corners give you previews.**

In a right-angled store a corner is binary. You see the next space or you do
not. In a crooked store with oblique angles, corners **reveal gradually** —
you get a widening sliver of the next room before you commit to entering it.

That is a real navigational gift, and it is unique to this geometry. Penhurst
is the store where you can look before you leap, and the price is that you
will never know where you are. A crew trades the map for the peek.

**Two entrances is a bigger change than it sounds:**

- Two extraction options, so the endgame becomes a **choice under pressure**
  rather than a single ritual. Which door, and is the other crew closer to the
  other one?
- The crew can split with a real reason to.
- And it cuts both ways: two ways in for things that are not the crew. Patrol
  routes can **loop** rather than dead-end, which no other anchor allows.

**The shop-in-shop is a room with its own rules.** A cosmetics concession has
its own lighting, its own ceiling, its own threshold — you step *into* another
store. Backlit product walls and vertical mirrors at eye height, which is the
**disorienting** kind of mirror rather than Boswell's plan-view kind. Penhurst
has mirrors and they do the opposite job.

**The salon is the single best horror asset in the set.** Mirrors facing each
other down a row of chairs. Basins. And hood dryers, which are head-shaped
silhouettes at head height, in rows, that do not move. `THE-STORE.md` already
has the Window Walkers standing where they worked, and the second floor's tell
is *one fewer figure than there was.* A row of hood dryers is that joke with
the setup pre-installed.

**Names needing a non-trademark:** the concession and the salon both want
period-plausible names. Suggestions, Chris's call — concession: **Verity**,
**Aurelle**, **Mirabeau Counter**. Salon: **Studio Nine**, **The Mezzanine
Salon**, or simply **Beauty Salon**, which is what most of them were actually
called and reads as more real than a clever name.

## Bellevue — the slot that completes the set

Unassigned so far, and there is an obvious gap: nothing in the set yet gives
knowledge **vertically**.

Boswell's gives you the plan. Sayers gives you the sequence. Penhurst gives
you the peek. Nobody gives you the **section** — the ability to see three
floors at once and understand the building as a stack.

So the proposal: **Bellevue is the grand one.** An open atrium or escalator
well running the full height, a main staircase, a tea room or restaurant on an
upper floor, railings and balconies overlooking the void.

**Signature affordance: vertical survey.** From a balcony you can see down
through the building. You learn where things are on *other floors* without
going to them — which is the only form of remote knowledge in the game that
does not involve another player.

And the cost writes itself: **if you can see down, you can be seen from
below.** A keeper with a lit lantern on a fourth-floor balcony is the most
visible object in the building. Bellevue's gift and Bellevue's danger are the
same hole in the middle of it.

That also makes it the natural home for the vertical ruin gradient already
written into `THE-STORE.md` — flood below, roof failure above, and a shaft
through the middle where you can see both at once.

## Why this matters more than flavour

A crew that has learned Boswell's walks into Sayers and finds their habit
gone. Not their map — their **method**. They were reading the ceiling; now
there is no ceiling worth reading and the answer is to shut up and listen.
Then Penhurst takes away the shape of the world and gives them a peek instead.
Then Bellevue gives them a view and charges them for standing in it.

That is four stores that are four games, built out of one kit of fixtures,
merchandise, ruin and one landmark apiece — the repetition-with-intent rule
from `THE-STORE.md` holding the whole time.

**The requirement this creates, restated because it is easy to lose:** the
universal tools — lantern projection, directory boards, the Fieldboard — must
be sufficient on their own. Every signature above is a bonus layer. A store
whose signature is load-bearing is a store that cannot be entered by someone
who learned the game somewhere else.
