# World systems board

Status: **living design board**, 2026-11-01. This is the short working map for
new lore, mechanics, and encounter ideas. It does not replace the binding
decision log: `DECISIONS.md` is canon authority; this board turns a promising
thought into a falsifiable playtest before it becomes a rule.

## The game in one sentence

At night, a Lightkeeper enters places still completing their last humane job.
Their lantern can read and interface with that work, but it also makes them a
visible, unrecognized person inside a live system.

The horror is not that machines hate people. It is that their procedures have
outlived the people and circumstances that made those procedures safe.

## The lantern is technical support, not a magic remote

The lantern is the Lightkeepers' standardized inspection instrument. It should
make the player feel capable *locally* without granting command of a building.
Every new lantern interaction must use one of these narrow verbs:

| Verb | What the player can do | What it must cost or risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Read** | Reveal a crate's department stamp, a route clue, a service label, or the state of a machine. | Light makes the player legible too. |
| **Present** | Offer a valid maintenance pattern at a local service port: a badge, routing tag, calibration mark, or work order. | It works only at authored infrastructure and may wake the procedure being inspected. |
| **Bias** | Redirect one simple behavior briefly: route a Sweeper toward a spill, send stock toward a pallet bay, open a maintenance hold. | The same procedure creates a new hazard, closes another route, or exposes the keeper. |
| **Trace** | Follow a power, goods, or service relationship from this room to a deeper place. | It gives knowledge and a destination, not a universal answer. |

The lantern cannot charm, stun, or make a machine friendly. If an interaction
solves a room without changing the player's position, light state, route, or
time pressure, it is too powerful.

## Fear: procedure creates the threat

The player should first want to leave, then learn enough to choose a deeper
risk. Encounters escalate by taking more control of the player's **space,
hands, route, or cargo**—not by merely adding health or damage.

| System | What it believes | Scary, readable consequence | Player answer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sweeper** | An aisle must be clear. | Knocks loose carried items, pushes a crate away, or sweeps the keeper toward an unsafe route. | Stay dark and out of its lane; create a louder obstruction elsewhere; retrieve dropped cargo later. |
| **Stocker / mannequin** | Inventory belongs in its assigned location. | Re-shelves a dropped bag, carries a loose object to a bay, or relocates an incapacitated keeper to a "holding" area. | Preserve the trail, break line of sight, use the lantern to read the destination, choose rescue versus extraction. |
| **Glimmer** | A fault needs reporting. | Tags the keeper and turns every later system into a response to that report. | Interrupt before the scan, hide, or make a decoy report elsewhere. |
| **Window Walker / Ficus** | A display or growing area needs light and order. | Holds a keeper beneath their own bright lantern until other procedures converge. | Go dark before contact, cut a route, or have a second keeper pull the scene apart. |
| **Warden** | Loss prevention. | Ends the fantasy that the building can be cleared; it converges on activity and forces departure. | Do not fight it. Leave, distract, or finish the extraction holdout. |

**No arbitrary bag deletion.** If cargo is dropped, moved, or shelved, it stays
in the world with a physical trail and a recoverable destination. The loss is
the route and time required to reclaim it. A forced relocation must likewise
leave the player in a playable space, not a cutscene that removes agency.

## Distraction is a survival language

The player needs ways to make a room unsafe somewhere other than where they
stand. These are per-run, found or crafted survival tools—not a weapon build.

| Tool | What it changes | Why it is frightening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thrown object / noisemaker | Creates an audible fault or obstruction at a chosen position. | It can summon the wrong procedure, and sound carries farther than intended. |
| Flare / temporary work light | Creates light somewhere the player is not. | It is a small sun: it reveals a route and feeds attention to the decoy location. |
| Arc tap / service snare | Holds a single procedure for seconds. | It buys passage, never victory; failure leaves the keeper beside the machine they stopped. |
| Loose pallet / cart / shutter | Changes the geometry using the building's own logistics. | The shortcut can become the next obstruction, and it may trap a teammate or cargo. |

None of these repels enemies. They redirect existing jobs. This preserves the
light rule and gives a desperate Resident-Evil-style decision: spend the thing
that might get you out now, or keep it for the room you have not seen yet.

## The first ten minutes: fear curriculum

1. **Ordinary wrongness:** a closed Boswell's still lights a checkout lane;
   the player hears a distant service loop and sees a pallet lane operating.
2. **Do not be seen working:** a Sweeper finishes a route in the dark. The
   player learns that its path is predictable and chooses to wait, sneak, or
   turn on the lantern to understand the next room.
3. **The first personal cost:** a needed crate or route clue requires raised
   lantern light and a hold. Something starts moving because of it.
4. **The first escape, not victory:** a procedure displaces the player's cargo
   or closes the expected exit. The player gets out by reading a service mark,
   taking a detour, or drawing it away—not by killing the room.
5. **The promise:** the recovered crate bears a stamp for a place the player
   has never visited. From a window, a distant light answers it. The store is
   enough for tonight; the valley becomes a future question.

## Expansion promises, not a content checklist

Every later place needs a distinct operational fantasy, a new connection to
the wider system, and one new survival option that changes how earlier places
are played. Do not build these before Boswell's passes the slice test.

| Place | Operational fantasy | What it can eventually give back |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rail yard | Goods are still routed and assembled. | Diversions, transport material, cargo routes, and a way to reassign shipments. |
| Foundry / data works | The town is still making replacement parts and scheduling work. | Components, maintenance access, and proof that the system is actively supplied. |
| Hospital | Triage and quarantine without living judgment. | Supplies, patient-routing knowledge, roof access/extraction promise. |
| Solar works | Power is collected and allocated by procedure. | A local control over which subsystems wake—not universal shutdown. |

The world may look like a robot society thriving, but it is not a civilization
with a secret king. It is infrastructure still relating correctly to itself and
incorrectly to humans.

### Lit floor — ambient light as a zone condition

```text
Idea:                 One authored room, and later one whole zone, is lit and
                      cannot be darkened. "Always under darkness" is a
                      condition, not a law.
Place / former job:   Boswell's: a display window bay or the checkout lane
                      that still lights itself. Later: an outdoor place, a
                      solar works, a platform under working floodlights.
Mistaken for:         Staff. A legible, badged presence doing legible work —
                      which is exactly what the keeper is not.
Consequence:          The keeper's own light stops being a decision. Every
                      procedure in the room has continuous reason to notice
                      them, and the lantern's Read verb gains nothing because
                      the room is already readable.
Counterplay:          Geometry and timing instead of darkness: cover, lanes,
                      cargo left outside the lit volume, a decoy that pulls
                      the room's attention to a position rather than a person.
                      Cutting the room's power is a Bias at a service port,
                      and it costs whatever that circuit was also doing.
Brings home:          That the dark was the advantage. A lit room should make
                      the player want their own darkness back.
First slice:          Boswell's, one room. Not a zone until the store passes.
Status:               inbox
```

**Why it matters more than it looks.** Row 5 says light reveals value and
attracts danger. A lit room the player cannot switch off is the only way to
make that rule *felt* rather than merely obeyed — it takes the choice away for
sixty seconds and hands it back. It is also the one idea on this page that can
break the game if it spreads: a second lit zone makes darkness the exception
and the core loop stops being a loop. **One room, then measure.**

### Contamination trail — the system follows evidence, not people

```text
Idea:                 The keeper leaves physical traces. Procedures respond to
                      the traces. The response escalates with the count, not
                      with aggression.
Place / former job:   Any. It is the honest mechanical form of "the building
                      treats a person as contamination."
Mistaken for:         Not mistaken for anything — that is the point. The
                      keeper is never classified as an intruder. A forced
                      door is a fault, wet tracks are a spill, a dropped
                      crate is misplaced stock. Each gets the correct job.
Consequence:          Cleaning and stocking work converges on where the
                      keeper has been. Routes the player used stop being
                      quiet. The pressure is retrospective: the room behind
                      you fills up.
Counterplay:          Leave less. Close what you opened, carry what you
                      dropped, choose a route you can afford to spoil. Or
                      spend evidence deliberately — a mess in the wrong aisle
                      is a decoy with a longer fuse than a thrown can.
Brings home:          Knowledge of your own footprint. A second run through
                      the same floor plays differently because you know what
                      marks it.
First slice:          Two trace types in Boswell's: a forced door and dropped
                      cargo. Nothing environmental until those read.
Status:               inbox
```

**What this fixes.** Row 47 says machines are indifferent, and indifference is
hard to feel in a chase. It is easy to feel when the thing pursuing you is a
*work order you generated*. This also gives the Glimmer's report a physical
sibling: one tags the person, one tags the place.

**What it must not become:** a stealth meter. No bar, no detection cone, no
"suspicion." The evidence is objects in the world and the player can look at
them.

### Escape that half-works — what each later place actually gives

```text
Idea:                 Every place after Boswell's offers a way out. None of
                      them is a rescue. Each one delivers something real and
                      smaller, and names the next place.
Place / former job:   Hospital: a helipad and a working radio, no fuel and no
                      pilot. Rail yard: fuel and routes, no clearance.
                      Foundry / data works: the clearance, at the cost of
                      whatever that scheduling capacity was holding up. Solar
                      works: the power to run any one of the above, not two.
Mistaken for:         A keeper requesting transport is an unscheduled
                      movement. The building files it correctly and nothing
                      arrives.
Consequence:          The campaign is a chain of near-exits. The danger of
                      this structure is feeling jerked around, so the rule is
                      hard: each station must actually deliver. The radio
                      works — you get a voice, not a helicopter. That is a
                      real reward and it is not the one you wanted.
Counterplay:          n/a — this is campaign shape, not an encounter.
Brings home:          A voice, a route, a schedule, a circuit. Four things
                      that only mean something together.
First slice:          None. This is the frame the zones hang on; it earns
                      nothing until Boswell's ships. The only part that
                      touches the slice is the wrong-stamped crate already on
                      this board, which is the first link in the chain.
Status:               inbox
```

**Why this is the right answer to "what is at the bottom."** The descent does
not end in a revelation about who is to blame. It ends in **authority** — the
ability to route power, fuel and clearance to exactly one place, which means
deciding what goes dark so that one thing can run. No confession, no villain,
and a final choice the player cannot hand to anyone else.

### Cross-zone topology — progress changes a place you already played

```text
Idea:                 Opening a route or reallocating power in a later place
                      changes what is reachable, lit, or awake in a place the
                      player has already finished.
Place / former job:   Solar works allocation; rail yard diversion; foundry
                      scheduling.
Mistaken for:         n/a — the keeper is operating infrastructure correctly,
                      with the lantern's Present and Bias verbs, at authored
                      ports.
Consequence:          Power is conserved. Waking a circuit starves another.
                      Starving a circuit can mean a group of machines never
                      comes on — and can also mean a shutter the player was
                      relying on no longer opens, or a route home goes dark.
Counterplay:          n/a — this is the meta-progression.
Brings home:          Reconfiguration instead of character upgrades. You do
                      not get stronger; the valley gets differently shaped.
First slice:          None. Requires two places.
Status:               inbox
```

**The economy is already in the game, one scale up.** Light is the currency at
the body scale: having it makes you capable and legible. Power is the same
currency at the valley scale: running one thing means something else is dark.
One idea at two scales is the difference between a designed game and an
assembled one — and it answers "what does more rail yard get you" with
*topology* rather than loot.

**The guardrail:** this is the second verb, and it must be learned by using it
at a port in act one, not unlocked as a reward in act three. A capability the
player is handed late reads as a quest-giver with the quest-giver removed.

## Two things the board already settles

**"Maybe lighting up certain ways does different things to the robots"** —
`DECISIONS.md` row 5 and the four-verb table above already close this. The
lantern Reads, Presents, Biases and Traces. It has no modes, colours or
frequencies, and a light that produces a specific machine response is the
light-repels-enemies exception wearing a costume. One light verb per chapter
at most, and each must be a different *relationship to being seen* rather
than a different beam.

**The Resident Evil feeling** — running past things because you are out of
options — comes from scarcity, and the lantern cannot supply it: row 10 makes
charge unlimited and that is locked. The scarcity lives in the distraction
tools above. For it to bite, they must be genuinely few, usable once, and
findable only in places worth the walk.

## One critique of the fear curriculum above

Step 1 is "ordinary wrongness" — a lit checkout lane, a distant service loop.
That is atmosphere, and atmosphere does not produce *I do not want to deal
with this.* It produces interest.

The first Sweeper encounter should not threaten the player at all. It should
finish its route, ignore them completely, and **take something they wanted** —
shelve the crate they were walking toward, or sweep it into a bay. Being
ignored by something that costs you is what makes a player decide to grab
their haul and leave, and it teaches row 47 in one beat with no dialogue.
Then the second encounter holds a door on them.

Aggression first teaches the player they are in a combat game. Indifference
first teaches them they are in this one.

### The chained blocker — and the way it goes wrong

```text
Idea:                 The radio is on the roof. You cannot reach the roof
                      because the elevator has no power; the power is out
                      because the basement is flooded; the pump needs parts;
                      Lanternhold itself needs fixing before it can hold
                      them.
Mistaken for:         A keeper restoring service is doing maintenance the
                      building agrees with — which is why this works at all.
Consequence:          Progress is a route being opened, not a counter being
                      filled.
Brings home:          Each fix is permanent and visible.
First slice:          Not Boswell's. This is the shape of act two onward.
Status:               inbox
```

**The failure mode, named now because it is the likely one.** If every link in
the chain is "find N of material X," the chain is a fetch quest with
set dressing, and row 29 already forbids that: progression is a ladder of
practical recovery jobs, never "collect ten glowing widgets."

**The rule that saves it: each blocker belongs to a different place.** Not a
different material — a different *building's* problem.

| Blocker | Whose problem it is |
| --- | --- |
| Elevator has no power | solar works — allocation, and something else goes dark |
| Basement is flooded | the pump run, and whatever the water was doing to the valley's over-engineering |
| Pump needs parts | foundry / data works — the town is still making replacements |
| Lanternhold cannot hold the parts yet | Lanternhold, and the first sink above |

Then the chain *is* the campaign, each link teaches one place, and
`cross-zone topology` is how the links connect rather than a separate system.
One chain, four places, no widget counters.

### The distress call makes it worse

```text
Idea:                 The radio works. The call goes out. Being heard is the
                      escalation — a deeper situation opens because you
                      transmitted, not because you found a document.
Mistaken for:         An unscheduled transmission on a monitored frequency.
                      Something still monitors it. Something answers.
Consequence:          The reward for progress is danger. New behaviour in
                      places already played; attention where there was none.
Counterplay:          Whether to transmit at all, and from where.
Brings home:          The fact that you are now known.
First slice:          None. It is the act-two hinge.
Status:               inbox
```

**This is the right shape and it passes row 38's test** — *a new level that is
not more dangerous than the last one is wallpaper.* Here the player
manufactures the danger themselves by succeeding, which is better than the
game handing it to them on a schedule.

**The one guardrail.** "A deeper plot unfurls" is exactly where
`THE-VALLEY.md` warns the story can collapse: into a confession, a villain, or
a mind that row 47 forbids. So —

> **What answers the radio is a procedure, not a person.**

Another automated system answers because answering is the last job it was
given. Two dead systems completing a protocol at each other is scarier than a
voice, and it costs nothing to explain. A keeper's transmission gets filed,
correctly, and what arrives is whatever the file says to send.

The plot that unfurls should be *legible and still unstoppable* — not a secret
being kept from the player.

## Inbox, 2026-11-01 — Chris, third pass: the pioneer arc

The macro shape. Reclaiming a valley that was yours, Lanternhold becoming
genuinely habitable — power, beds, food, plants — and an endgame where the
valley is taken back.

### Reclamation gives the keepers a claim

`GAME-THESIS.md` already describes this arc: *"a pump runs, a route opens, a
record survives, a hall gets warmer, and eventually a city can remember
itself."* The pioneer framing is that sentence with a verb, so it is not new
scope.

What it adds is better than a theme. It gives the keeper a **claim**, and the
claim is contested by the only party that keeps records:

> The keeper believes the valley is theirs. The building's inventory says
> otherwise. **Both are correct**, and row 47 is why — the machines are not
> wrong about anything. They are completing the last job they were given, and
> that job has a stock count in it.

This is stronger than making the machines mistaken. A keeper carrying property
out of a building that is still doing loss prevention is not a hero fighting a
monster; they are a person with an older claim than the paperwork.

### Habitability is capability, never a consumable

```text
Idea:                 Power, beds, food, growing space. Lanternhold becomes a
                      place people can actually live, by stages.
Consequence:          Each stage changes what the crew can ATTEMPT next run —
                      longer nights out, more keepers at once, another lantern
                      on the wall.
Status:               inbox
```

**Chris's own guardrail, and it is the right one:** *"not like you should have
a depleted food or Minecraft survival method."* That instinct matches a locked
decision. Row 10 removed the lantern's fuel gauge for the same reason — *"the
risk is always having it on."* A hunger meter is that mistake in a different
slot: attrition without a decision.

`GAME-THESIS.md` already states the rule this follows: **the durable reward is
capability and visible restoration; the disposable reward is the haul from
this night.** So beds, power and a kitchen are permanent and they are never
topped up.

### What growth buys is people

The population is the progress bar, and it is better than a number because the
display already exists: `HubService`'s **Lamp Wall** shows one lantern per
keeper out, lit, taken down, or put out.

More beds and food mean more keepers. More keepers mean more light in the
valley, and more light means more attention. **The hub's growth feeds the
thing that makes the nights harder** — which is light-as-a-rate again, one
scale up, and it means the reward for progress is danger without the game
having to arrange that on a schedule.

### The endgame — and why it is not reprogramming

Chris: *"maybe eventually we do reclaim everything or reprogram the robots or
whatever."* The instinct is right that the arc needs a destination that feels
like victory. **Reprogramming cannot be it**, on two grounds already in the
repo:

1. **It is row 5's banned exception at campaign scale.** The lantern Reads,
   Presents, Biases and Traces, and the board states it *"cannot charm, stun,
   or make a machine friendly."* A reprogram deletes the only decision the
   game has, permanently.
2. **If the machines can be reprogrammed, they were always a solvable
   technical problem** — and the indifference thesis was a stall rather than
   the truth. The ending would retroactively make the whole premise a puzzle
   box.

The version that keeps the premise and still lands as victory:

> **You are not reprogrammed. You are re-filed.**

The system's problem with a keeper was never hostility. It is that a keeper is
an *unscheduled person* — unrecognized, uncounted, correctly handled as a
fault. Victory is becoming **scheduled**: the paperwork now says the valley
produces for Lanternhold, so it does. The town starts shipping to you.

This costs no new mechanic. It is the wrong-stamped crate and the service
port, inverted — the same two objects already on this board, read the other
way round.

And it keeps the horror, which an ending must do or the game changes genre in
its last hour. Being inside the system means the system's errors are now about
you. A crate arrives that nobody ordered. A sweeper holds a door *for* you.
**The thing that was indifferent is now indifferent on your behalf**, and that
is worse than being hunted.

### What persists when a run is lost

Row 8 takes the whole carried haul on a wipe or an expired timer.
`GAME-THESIS.md` requires that *"a loss must hurt without making a player feel
their evening was erased."*

The pioneer frame resolves those cleanly: **you lose the haul and you keep the
knowledge.** Which route floods, which lane a Sweeper owns, which stamp points
where, what marks a floor. Row 43 makes the store the tutorial;
`KNOWLEDGE-OF-PLACE.md` makes a location teach time and space. A failed run
that taught you the building is still progress, and nothing has to be granted
to make that true.

### The discipline this needs, because base-building eats games

Rows 3, 28 and 34 all forbid expanding before the slice passes, and a growing
hub is the most appetite-driven system a game can have: every fixture wants
art, UI and balance.

One test, the hub-side mirror of row 38's test for zones:

> **A new Lanternhold fixture that does not change what the crew can attempt
> on the next run is wallpaper.**

And the first move is not a shop. `PROGRESSION-GAP.md` has five fixtures
already placed, named and signed, saying "not open yet," with four declared
sinks that have no consumer. **Wire one.** That is the whole of this idea's
first slice.

## Endings — candidates, and the test they have to pass

Chris, 2026-11-01: *"there is probably a much better ending that adverse
hasn't found yet. The most sinister and horrifying possibilities."* He is
right, and the previous entry's ending is withdrawn as the recommendation.

### Why "re-filed" failed

It is a structural ending, not a horrifying one. Named so the same mistake is
recognizable next time:

- It **resolves** the tension instead of charging for it. Becoming scheduled
  is a solution; horror is a cost.
- It is a reveal about the **world**, not about the player. The valley turns
  out to work differently than you thought. Nothing you did is implicated.
- Every hour already played stays **innocent**, so the ending cannot reach
  backwards. It is a last scene rather than a reinterpretation.

### The test

An ending for this game has to pass all six.

| | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | **It implicates the hours already played.** Not a fact about the valley — a re-reading of what the player has been doing all game |
| 2 | **Nobody is evil.** Row 47. No villain, no secret king, no appetite |
| 3 | **No new mechanic.** The evidence is something the player has handled for twenty hours without seeing it |
| 4 | **The staff stay right.** The people who tell you things are not lying and are not fools |
| 5 | **It does not stage the real disaster.** `THE-VALLEY.md` |
| 6 | **It is legible before it happens.** The player is told, understands, and continues. Tragedy, not a gotcha |

"Re-filed" passes 2, 3, 5 and fails 1 and 6.

### Candidate A — the keepers are the valley's maintenance

The machines stock, clean, contain, route and restrain. **None of them can
improvise.** They cannot decide to drain a basement, divert a shipment, or
reroute a circuit. A keeper can. So every "practical recovery job" in row 29's
ladder was maintenance the valley needed and could not perform on itself.

This is why the lantern works at all. It **Presents a valid maintenance
pattern at a service port** and the system accepts it — and the reason a dead
system accepts a pattern is that it was built expecting a maintenance
instrument to arrive. The lantern is not a tool the keepers invented to
survive the valley. It is the valley's own interface, and the order has been
holding the contractor's end of a contract nobody alive signed.

Passes all six. The evidence is the service port, used since act one. The
reinterpretation is total: the progress bar was theirs.

### Candidate B — what you are dismantling is a defense

The valley is over-engineered against water because something already
happened to it. The schedule is **correct**. The disaster it prepares for is
real and still coming, and the preparation is proportionate.

Reclaiming the valley means turning that infrastructure to your purposes,
which means it is no longer doing its job. The pioneer arc is a disarmament.

This is the false all-clear at campaign scale, and it is the most Johnstown
idea available without staging a flood: **the town that over-prepared was
right.** Passes 5 because the disaster is ahead, not behind — nobody's death
is content.

Requires 6 to be honored absolutely. The staff must say this out loud, in act
two, clearly, and be believed or not. Without that it is a gotcha that
invalidates the play.

### Candidate C — Lanternhold is a building, too

The hub is a place completing the last job it was built to do, and its job is
**keeping keepers going out.** Beds exist to hold the next shift. Food exists
to make the shift viable. The Lamp Wall — one lantern per keeper out, lit,
taken down, put out — is not a memorial. It is a shift board, and the player
has been reading it correctly all along without noticing what kind of document
it is.

Every upgrade in the pioneer arc increased a facility's throughput.

Row 47 applied to us, which is the only place it has not been applied yet. The
discipline that keeps it honest: **the people of Lanternhold are not lying and
are not cynical.** The institution outlived the reason, the way every other
institution in this valley did. That is the same thesis, not an exception to
it.

Passes all six, and it is the one that lands at home rather than in a dungeon.

### Candidate D — the filed keepers are the current shift

`CONTAINED-KEEPERS.md` holds the rescue contract. The sinister reading: the
filed were not stored. They were **assigned.**

Some of what moves in the dark with purpose, carrying light, is previous
keepers still completing a last job. It is why they recognize you and why you
recognize them. A Window Walker holding someone beneath their own bright
lantern is a keeper doing a keeper's work.

**The guardrail that keeps this inside row 47:** the system does not want
flesh and never did — row 47 is explicit. It wants the post filled. A person
is simply what was available. The moment this reads as hunger it has broken
the premise and becomes a zombie.

Rescue becomes the hardest thing in the game: you can bring one home, and
what comes home is a worker.

### Candidate E — restoration only relocates harm

Not an ending on its own; the mechanism that makes the others hurt.

**There is no net repair, only displacement.** Drain the basement and the
water goes somewhere. Route power and something else goes dark — already true
as cross-zone topology, now with moral weight. Divert a shipment and a place
you have never been stops receiving.

For a valley shaped by water, *it has to go somewhere* is the cheapest and
truest horror available, and it needs no reveal at all. The player can watch
it happen in act one.

### Recommendation — A as the spine, B as the bottom, C as the turn home

They are one story, not five options:

1. **A** is what the whole campaign has been: the keepers are the valley's
   hands, and the lantern is the badge.
2. **E** is how it is felt, every night, from act one: nothing is ever
   repaired, only moved.
3. **C** is the moment it reaches Lanternhold: the hub is running a rota, and
   the Lamp Wall was always a shift board.
4. **B** is the bottom of the descent: the thing you are taking back is a
   defense, and the only way to own it is to stop it working.
5. **D** is the texture you walk past for twenty hours before any of it is
   said.

The final decision is then unbearable and nobody is evil: **the only way to
reclaim the valley is to stop it doing the job it was built for, and the job
was never wrong.** No confession, no villain, no mind. The player supplies the
guilt, which is the one kind that lands.

**Status: candidates.** This is not a decision and no part of it enters
`DECISIONS.md` without Chris. Nothing here is built before Boswell's passes
the slice test — rows 3, 28, 34.

## Inbox, 2026-11-01 — two what-ifs, parked with a reason

Chris raised these and deprioritized them himself in the same breath — *"what
if this what if that. But yea. Let's at least get the horrifying first mini
play through done."* Logged so they are not re-derived, judged so the judgement
is not re-derived either.

### "What if you are a robot yourself, and you never knew"

**The strongest objection is not that it is a cliché.** It is that it breaks
the one rule holding this premise together.

Row 47: the machines are indifferent, and they are indifferent because their
procedures outlived the conditions that made them humane. A keeper who turns
out to be a machine makes the player *a member of that category* — and the
moment the player belongs to it, the question "what do they want?" has an
answer again, because now one of them wants something. The indifference stops
being structural and becomes a thing the plot made an exception to.

It also cancels the mechanic the game is built on. **Light makes you legible
because you are an unscheduled person.** If a keeper is a machine, the lantern
is not a credential carried by an outsider; it is just equipment, and the
central tension — the only way to work is to be visible — loses its reason.

Worth keeping from it: the *fear* of it. A keeper who cannot be sure, because
the building keeps filing them correctly, is free. A keeper who turns out to
be right is not.

### "What if an army comes and takes over, and the robots become a tertiary party"

Three-way is a real and underused shape, and the instinct behind it is sound:
the valley should not be the only pressure forever.

**But it changes genre.** An army is a faction with intent, which means
negotiation, territory and sides — and every one of those is a thing the
machines cannot do. Put a party with motives on the board and the party
without motives becomes the *environment*, not the antagonist. Row 47 survives
in letter and dies in practice, because the player's attention moves to the
only thing that can be reasoned with.

**The version that keeps the premise:** whatever arrives does not have
motives either. Another system, another schedule, with a different last job
and no awareness that the first one is running. Two procedures meeting in one
valley, each correct, neither aware. The keepers are what gets caught between
them — which is a three-way without a single mind added to the fiction, and it
is the same move as "what answers the radio is a procedure, not a person."

That is also the honest sequel hook for candidate B: if what you disarmed was
a defense, **something it was defending against is a system too.**

**Status: parked.** Neither is built, neither is canon, and neither enters
`DECISIONS.md`. Both are act-three-or-later questions and rows 3, 28 and 34
all say the store comes first.
