# World systems board

Status: **living design board**, 2026-11-01. This is the short working map for
new lore, mechanics, and encounter ideas. It does not replace the binding
decision log: `DECISIONS.md` is canon authority; this board turns a promising
thought into a falsifiable playtest before it becomes a rule.

## The game in one sentence

At night, a Lightkeeper enters places still completing their last humane job.
Their lantern can read and interface with that work, but it also makes them a
visible, unrecognized person inside a live system.

The horror is not that machines hate people. It is that their procedures have
outlived the people and circumstances that made those procedures safe.

## The lantern is technical support, not a magic remote

The lantern is the Lightkeepers' standardized inspection instrument. It should
make the player feel capable *locally* without granting command of a building.
Every new lantern interaction must use one of these narrow verbs:

| Verb | What the player can do | What it must cost or risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Read** | Reveal a crate's department stamp, a route clue, a service label, or the state of a machine. | Light makes the player legible too. |
| **Present** | Offer a valid maintenance pattern at a local service port: a badge, routing tag, calibration mark, or work order. | It works only at authored infrastructure and may wake the procedure being inspected. |
| **Bias** | Redirect one simple behavior briefly: route a Sweeper toward a spill, send stock toward a pallet bay, open a maintenance hold. | The same procedure creates a new hazard, closes another route, or exposes the keeper. |
| **Trace** | Follow a power, goods, or service relationship from this room to a deeper place. | It gives knowledge and a destination, not a universal answer. |

The lantern cannot charm, stun, or make a machine friendly. If an interaction
solves a room without changing the player's position, light state, route, or
time pressure, it is too powerful.

## Fear: procedure creates the threat

The player should first want to leave, then learn enough to choose a deeper
risk. Encounters escalate by taking more control of the player's **space,
hands, route, or cargo**—not by merely adding health or damage.

| System | What it believes | Scary, readable consequence | Player answer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Sweeper** | An aisle must be clear. | Knocks loose carried items, pushes a crate away, or sweeps the keeper toward an unsafe route. | Stay dark and out of its lane; create a louder obstruction elsewhere; retrieve dropped cargo later. |
| **Stocker / mannequin** | Inventory belongs in its assigned location. | Re-shelves a dropped bag, carries a loose object to a bay, or relocates an incapacitated keeper to a "holding" area. | Preserve the trail, break line of sight, use the lantern to read the destination, choose rescue versus extraction. |
| **Glimmer** | A fault needs reporting. | Tags the keeper and turns every later system into a response to that report. | Interrupt before the scan, hide, or make a decoy report elsewhere. |
| **Window Walker / Ficus** | A display or growing area needs light and order. | Holds a keeper beneath their own bright lantern until other procedures converge. | Go dark before contact, cut a route, or have a second keeper pull the scene apart. |
| **Warden** | Loss prevention. | Ends the fantasy that the building can be cleared; it converges on activity and forces departure. | Do not fight it. Leave, distract, or finish the extraction holdout. |

**No arbitrary bag deletion.** If cargo is dropped, moved, or shelved, it stays
in the world with a physical trail and a recoverable destination. The loss is
the route and time required to reclaim it. A forced relocation must likewise
leave the player in a playable space, not a cutscene that removes agency.

## Distraction is a survival language

The player needs ways to make a room unsafe somewhere other than where they
stand. These are per-run, found or crafted survival tools—not a weapon build.

| Tool | What it changes | Why it is frightening |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thrown object / noisemaker | Creates an audible fault or obstruction at a chosen position. | It can summon the wrong procedure, and sound carries farther than intended. |
| Flare / temporary work light | Creates light somewhere the player is not. | It is a small sun: it reveals a route and feeds attention to the decoy location. |
| Arc tap / service snare | Holds a single procedure for seconds. | It buys passage, never victory; failure leaves the keeper beside the machine they stopped. |
| Loose pallet / cart / shutter | Changes the geometry using the building's own logistics. | The shortcut can become the next obstruction, and it may trap a teammate or cargo. |

None of these repels enemies. They redirect existing jobs. This preserves the
light rule and gives a desperate Resident-Evil-style decision: spend the thing
that might get you out now, or keep it for the room you have not seen yet.

## The first ten minutes: fear curriculum

1. **Ordinary wrongness:** a closed Boswell's still lights a checkout lane;
   the player hears a distant service loop and sees a pallet lane operating.
2. **Do not be seen working:** a Sweeper finishes a route in the dark. The
   player learns that its path is predictable and chooses to wait, sneak, or
   turn on the lantern to understand the next room.
3. **The first personal cost:** a needed crate or route clue requires raised
   lantern light and a hold. Something starts moving because of it.
4. **The first escape, not victory:** a procedure displaces the player's cargo
   or closes the expected exit. The player gets out by reading a service mark,
   taking a detour, or drawing it away—not by killing the room.
5. **The promise:** the recovered crate bears a stamp for a place the player
   has never visited. From a window, a distant light answers it. The store is
   enough for tonight; the valley becomes a future question.

## Expansion promises, not a content checklist

Every later place needs a distinct operational fantasy, a new connection to
the wider system, and one new survival option that changes how earlier places
are played. Do not build these before Boswell's passes the slice test.

| Place | Operational fantasy | What it can eventually give back |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rail yard | Goods are still routed and assembled. | Diversions, transport material, cargo routes, and a way to reassign shipments. |
| Foundry / data works | The town is still making replacement parts and scheduling work. | Components, maintenance access, and proof that the system is actively supplied. |
| Hospital | Triage and quarantine without living judgment. | Supplies, patient-routing knowledge, roof access/extraction promise. |
| Solar works | Power is collected and allocated by procedure. | A local control over which subsystems wake—not universal shutdown. |

The world may look like a robot society thriving, but it is not a civilization
with a secret king. It is infrastructure still relating correctly to itself and
incorrectly to humans.

## How new ideas enter the game

Add an idea here first as a **pressure test**, then move it only after a
playtest proves it earns its place:

```text
Idea:

What the system mistakes the keeper for:
Immediate physical consequence:

What the player brings home or learns:

Status: inbox | proposed | tested | canon | cut
```

An idea is ready for a build ticket only if it answers all six lines. A lore
idea is ready for canon only if a player can encounter it through action,
environment, or a recovered thing—not an explanation screen.

## Working discipline

- `DECISIONS.md`: binding calls only. Do not quietly promote a board idea.
- This board: current mechanics/lore synthesis and pressure tests.
- `VERTICAL-SLICE.md`: only what must ship in the 10–15 minute proof.
- `ZONES.md`: later-place contracts only after the store loop works.
- `COORDINATION.md`: ownership, file seams, and what is actively being built.

### Lit floor — ambient light as a zone condition

```text
Idea:                 One authored room, and later one whole zone, is lit and
                      cannot be darkened. "Always under darkness" is a
                      condition, not a law.
Place / former job:   Boswell's: a display window bay or the checkout lane
                      that still lights itself. Later: an outdoor place, a
                      solar works, a platform under working floodlights.
Mistaken for:         Staff. A legible, badged presence doing legible work —
                      which is exactly what the keeper is not.
Consequence:          The keeper's own light stops being a decision. Every
                      procedure in the room has continuous reason to notice
                      them, and the lantern's Read verb gains nothing because
                      the room is already readable.
Counterplay:          Geometry and timing instead of darkness: cover, lanes,
                      cargo left outside the lit volume, a decoy that pulls
                      the room's attention to a position rather than a person.
                      Cutting the room's power is a Bias at a service port,
                      and it costs whatever that circuit was also doing.
Brings home:          That the dark was the advantage. A lit room should make
                      the player want their own darkness back.
First slice:          Boswell's, one room. Not a zone until the store passes.
Status:               inbox
```

**Why it matters more than it looks.** Row 5 says light reveals value and
attracts danger. A lit room the player cannot switch off is the only way to
make that rule *felt* rather than merely obeyed — it takes the choice away for
sixty seconds and hands it back. It is also the one idea on this page that can
break the game if it spreads: a second lit zone makes darkness the exception
and the core loop stops being a loop. **One room, then measure.**

### Contamination trail — the system follows evidence, not people

```text
Idea:                 The keeper leaves physical traces. Procedures respond to
                      the traces. The response escalates with the count, not
                      with aggression.
Place / former job:   Any. It is the honest mechanical form of "the building
                      treats a person as contamination."
Mistaken for:         Not mistaken for anything — that is the point. The
                      keeper is never classified as an intruder. A forced
                      door is a fault, wet tracks are a spill, a dropped
                      crate is misplaced stock. Each gets the correct job.
Consequence:          Cleaning and stocking work converges on where the
                      keeper has been. Routes the player used stop being
                      quiet. The pressure is retrospective: the room behind
                      you fills up.
Counterplay:          Leave less. Close what you opened, carry what you
                      dropped, choose a route you can afford to spoil. Or
                      spend evidence deliberately — a mess in the wrong aisle
                      is a decoy with a longer fuse than a thrown can.
Brings home:          Knowledge of your own footprint. A second run through
                      the same floor plays differently because you know what
                      marks it.
First slice:          Two trace types in Boswell's: a forced door and dropped
                      cargo. Nothing environmental until those read.
Status:               inbox
```

**What this fixes.** Row 47 says machines are indifferent, and indifference is
hard to feel in a chase. It is easy to feel when the thing pursuing you is a
*work order you generated*. This also gives the Glimmer's report a physical
sibling: one tags the person, one tags the place.

**What it must not become:** a stealth meter. No bar, no detection cone, no
"suspicion." The evidence is objects in the world and the player can look at
them.

### Escape that half-works — what each later place actually gives

```text
Idea:                 Every place after Boswell's offers a way out. None of
                      them is a rescue. Each one delivers something real and
                      smaller, and names the next place.
Place / former job:   Hospital: a helipad and a working radio, no fuel and no
                      pilot. Rail yard: fuel and routes, no clearance.
                      Foundry / data works: the clearance, at the cost of
                      whatever that scheduling capacity was holding up. Solar
                      works: the power to run any one of the above, not two.
Mistaken for:         A keeper requesting transport is an unscheduled
                      movement. The building files it correctly and nothing
                      arrives.
Consequence:          The campaign is a chain of near-exits. The danger of
                      this structure is feeling jerked around, so the rule is
                      hard: each station must actually deliver. The radio
                      works — you get a voice, not a helicopter. That is a
                      real reward and it is not the one you wanted.
Counterplay:          n/a — this is campaign shape, not an encounter.
Brings home:          A voice, a route, a schedule, a circuit. Four things
                      that only mean something together.
First slice:          None. This is the frame the zones hang on; it earns
                      nothing until Boswell's ships. The only part that
                      touches the slice is the wrong-stamped crate already on
                      this board, which is the first link in the chain.
Status:               inbox
```

**Why this is the right answer to "what is at the bottom."** The descent does
not end in a revelation about who is to blame. It ends in **authority** — the
ability to route power, fuel and clearance to exactly one place, which means
deciding what goes dark so that one thing can run. No confession, no villain,
and a final choice the player cannot hand to anyone else.

### Cross-zone topology — progress changes a place you already played

```text
Idea:                 Opening a route or reallocating power in a later place
                      changes what is reachable, lit, or awake in a place the
                      player has already finished.
Place / former job:   Solar works allocation; rail yard diversion; foundry
                      scheduling.
Mistaken for:         n/a — the keeper is operating infrastructure correctly,
                      with the lantern's Present and Bias verbs, at authored
                      ports.
Consequence:          Power is conserved. Waking a circuit starves another.
                      Starving a circuit can mean a group of machines never
                      comes on — and can also mean a shutter the player was
                      relying on no longer opens, or a route home goes dark.
Counterplay:          n/a — this is the meta-progression.
Brings home:          Reconfiguration instead of character upgrades. You do
                      not get stronger; the valley gets differently shaped.
First slice:          None. Requires two places.
Status:               inbox
```

**The economy is already in the game, one scale up.** Light is the currency at
the body scale: having it makes you capable and legible. Power is the same
currency at the valley scale: running one thing means something else is dark.
One idea at two scales is the difference between a designed game and an
assembled one — and it answers "what does more rail yard get you" with
*topology* rather than loot.

**The guardrail:** this is the second verb, and it must be learned by using it
at a port in act one, not unlocked as a reward in act three. A capability the
player is handed late reads as a quest-giver with the quest-giver removed.

## Two things the board already settles

**"Maybe lighting up certain ways does different things to the robots"** —
`DECISIONS.md` row 5 and the four-verb table above already close this. The
lantern Reads, Presents, Biases and Traces. It has no modes, colours or
frequencies, and a light that produces a specific machine response is the
light-repels-enemies exception wearing a costume. One light verb per chapter
at most, and each must be a different *relationship to being seen* rather
than a different beam.

**The Resident Evil feeling** — running past things because you are out of
options — comes from scarcity, and the lantern cannot supply it: row 10 makes
charge unlimited and that is locked. The scarcity lives in the distraction
tools above. For it to bite, they must be genuinely few, usable once, and
findable only in places worth the walk.

## One critique of the fear curriculum above

Step 1 is "ordinary wrongness" — a lit checkout lane, a distant service loop.
That is atmosphere, and atmosphere does not produce *I do not want to deal
with this.* It produces interest.

The first Sweeper encounter should not threaten the player at all. It should
finish its route, ignore them completely, and **take something they wanted** —
shelve the crate they were walking toward, or sweep it into a bay. Being
ignored by something that costs you is what makes a player decide to grab
their haul and leave, and it teaches row 47 in one beat with no dialogue.
Then the second encounter holds a door on them.

Aggression first teaches the player they are in a combat game. Indifference
first teaches them they are in this one.
