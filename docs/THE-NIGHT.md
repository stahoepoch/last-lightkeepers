# The night, the storm, and what the machines want

Status: Chris's lore is **his, 2026-10-28**, and the four things he stated are
recorded as `DECISIONS.md` rows 45 and 46. Everything below marked *proposed*
is Claude's derivation and awaits his word — including the answer to his
question, which is the last section and is the most consequential proposal in
this document.

What he said:

> *"The storm comes every night, we need to get in and out of wherever before
> the storm gets too bad. The earth is ravaged by storms at night that wake up
> the machines, we have a limited window to get in and out. The mall will
> flood, eventually we can add so much more to stop flooding and really fix the
> whole situation. End goal, rebuild civilization. Find the library downtown,
> recover information, museum, history. Reclaim the past. What do the machines
> want humans for, why do they hate us?"*

## First, the thing this fixes

**Storm Charge was the last major mechanic in the game with no fiction behind
it.** It rose, it never fell, light made it rise faster, and at thresholds the
world got worse. Every one of those is a good rule and none of them meant
anything. `PREMISE.md` explained the dark, the lantern, the solar farm and the
Window Walkers, and left the meter as a number that goes up.

Now the meter *is* the night's storm arriving. "Only ever rises" stops being a
balance rule and becomes a fact about weather: a storm does not un-arrive.

That is the single most valuable thing in his message, and it was free.

## The window, and why it is not a contradiction

There is an apparent collision here that has to be handled rather than
smoothed, because `PREMISE.md` says the opposite of what his message implies.

- **`PREMISE.md`:** in daylight the machines are fed — "all of them awake,
  fast, everywhere," so nobody goes. At night they run on reserve, patrols
  slow, some stand dormant where they stopped. **Night is the gap.**
- **Chris, 2026-10-28:** storms ravage the earth at night and **wake the
  machines.**

Read carelessly, one says night is safe and the other says night is when they
wake. Read properly, the second completes the first, because `PREMISE.md` never
said why the gap *ends*:

**The night is the window. The storm is the window closing.**

Dusk is the best moment of the day — the sun is gone, their reserves are low,
and dormant units stand where they stopped. Then the storm comes, as it comes
every night, and it brings the one thing the dead grid cannot: enormous
electrical energy. The building fills with power that is not yours. They come
up one by one.

So the run is bounded at both ends by the same weather. You go in when the
light fails and you leave before the storm peaks, and the meter on your HUD is
simply **how much of tonight's storm has arrived.**

Nothing in `PREMISE.md` needs editing. It answered *why not daytime* and this
answers *why not all night*, which nobody had asked.

## The meter has two inputs, and that is the whole game — *proposed*

If Storm Charge is the storm, a lantern cannot hasten it; weather does not care
about your lamp. So the meter is not the storm itself. It is **charge in the
machines**, and it has two sources:

| Source | Whose | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The storm | Nobody's | Inevitable, scheduled, rising all night |
| Your lantern | Yours | Optional, local, immediate |

Which means the sentence the whole design has been reaching for:

> **Every time you light the lantern, you do to yourself, early, what the
> storm is going to do to you anyway.**

You are a small storm. That is why the curve is `diminishing` rather than
free, why four lanterns cost more than one, and why going dark slows the climb
without ever reversing it — you can stop adding, but you cannot unplug the sky.

The name was right from the first day. Nobody had noticed it was doing two jobs.

## The flood — *proposed*

He said the mall will flood. The strongest version is not a state the building
is in; it is **something the storm does while you are inside it.**

Water rises through the night. It starts in the basement, where
`THE-STORE.md` already keeps it, and it climbs — so the richest floor in the
game is the first one the night takes away, and the Components you came for are
under the part of the building that drowns first.

This gives the meter a **physical twin**. The number on the HUD and the water on
the stairs are the same information, which is row 16's two-channel rule
arriving in the level itself rather than in an enemy cue.

**It is not a second timer, and it must never become one.** One clock, shown
two ways. If the water ever runs on its own schedule the game has two deadlines
and the player can serve neither.

### Stopping it, and the cost nobody has priced

He said: *eventually we can add so much more to stop flooding and really fix
the whole situation.* That is the restoration loop pointed at the world instead
of the hall, and it is a genuinely new progression axis — not more floor, not
more danger, but **the place getting better because you fixed it.**

The tension to settle before anyone builds it: `THE-STORE.md`'s ruin gradient
is the art direction *and*, since row 43, the tutorial curriculum. Lesson five
is the basement — *sometimes the choice is made for you*, because there is no
ambient light down there at all. Drain the basement and the lesson survives;
the water does not.

The cheap resolution, and it is probably right: **pumps hold the water back
during a run, they do not remove it.** You buy *time in the basement*, not a dry
basement. The floor stays black, the stock stays ruined, and what you earned is
twenty more minutes before the night takes it. Restoration as a reprieve rather
than a deletion — which is also the only version that does not eventually
delete the building's best floor.

## What it is all for

> *"End goal, rebuild civilization. Find the library downtown, recover
> information, museum, history. Reclaim the past."*

This raises the ladder's ceiling. `ZONES.md` ends at the city with the old hall
relit, and that stays — but relighting the hall is now a **step**, not the
summit. The summit is knowledge.

It also, without trying, makes the order's own founding fact into the game's
whole purpose. The Lightkeepers have kept records since the first flood. The
city kept its records in a library. **Reclaiming the library is the Keeping
Room at civic scale** — the same work, four zones further in, for everyone
instead of for one order.

And it completes a pairing the relics had been carrying alone:

| | What it recovers |
| --- | --- |
| **Relics** | Who we were. One person, two sentences, on a plinth |
| **The library and the museum** | What we knew. The things nobody's grandchild remembers |

### One rule this must not break — *proposed*

`REWARDS.md` forbids a fifth currency, and *information* is exactly the shape
of thing that becomes one by accident. So recovered knowledge is **capability,
never currency**: it does not stack, it is not spent, and there is no counter
for it. You recover a document and the hall can now do something it could not
do before.

Which closes his own loop neatly: **the library is where you learn to stop the
flooding.** Not a shopping list — a thing you did not know, found in the place
that knew it.

## His question, answered — and then corrected

**LOCKED, row 47.** The core answer stood; its *scope* did not, and Chris's
correction is the better rule. Recorded that way round because the record
should show which part was wrong.

My version was *you are a person removing merchandise from a store at night*.
His objection, exactly right: **"That only explains the mall."** A rule derived
from one building had quietly become the fiction of a six-zone ladder.

His rule:

> **The storm wakes broken infrastructure, and every place tries to finish the
> last job it was built to do.** A Lightkeeper becomes dangerous because they
> are *an unrecognized person inside an active system* — carrying equipment
> away, entering restricted areas, turning on power, or disrupting an emergency
> protocol.

**They do not hate people and do not need people.** They are executing
degraded maintenance, safety, stocking and loss-prevention instructions in a
building that never received a shutdown order. In Chris's words: *"their idea
of 'correcting' you is just often lethal in a ruined world."*

A Sweeper sweeps. You are debris on its floor. A Stocker stocks, and you are
stock in the wrong place. A Glimmer was maintenance — it finds a fault and
reports it, which is the scan and the tag, already built. The Window Walker
stood in a display window being looked at, and now walks toward whatever is
lit. The Ficus was landscaping, and it has grown.

**Nothing in that list is malice. All of it is correct behaviour.** It is also
not a new idea — it is Chris's own locked rule, *enemies come from the job the
place did*, taken to its conclusion. The roster already believes this answer.

Everything they do to you is procedurally right. You are not hunted; you are
**processed.**

### The boundary, and it is the discipline that makes this work

Chris's rule, and nothing in the roster may breach it:

> **The machines do not need a murder motive. They contain, remove, restrain,
> reroute, retrieve and lock down.**

The flood, the storm, the dark, the collapsing infrastructure and the inability
to extract are what make those verbs dangerous. **The lethality lives in the
ruin, not in the machine** — which is a far harder discipline than writing
monsters, and it is why this fiction can carry six zones without becoming a
bestiary.

It also has a mechanical consequence worth noticing: an enemy's primary output
is not damage. It is a *state* — tagged, restrained, isolated, cut off, locked
in, relocated. `data/enemies.json` gives five of the six a health value that
nothing in the game can reduce, a contradiction `COMBAT.md` has been carrying
since 2026-10-26. Row 47 says those numbers were never the point.

### What each place thinks it is doing — Chris's table

| Zone | What the place thinks it is doing | What it does to a Lightkeeper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mall** | Prevent loss; clean and restock | Recovers inventory, clears "debris," locks down exits |
| **Hospital** | Triage, quarantine, protect patients | Tags, restrains, isolates, transports you to unsafe wards |
| **Train station** | Keep people off tracks; route evacuations | Closes gates, redirects platforms, sends maintenance units, moves trains |
| **Downtown** | Maintain utilities and disaster response | Barricades streets, runs pumps, reroutes power, treats people as obstacles |
| **Library / museum** | Preserve records and artifacts | Locks collections down, returns items to storage, protects rooms from "contamination" |
| **Solar farm** | Protect a dangerous power site | Enforces exclusion zones and directs enormous charge into active systems |

**Every new map now arrives with a design question attached**, which is the
part that makes this generative rather than decorative:

> *What was this building built to protect or keep running — and why does a
> Lightkeeper violate that job?*

And a test for whether the answer was done honestly, in his words: a hospital
enemy *"should not feel like a mall security guard in scrubs; it should feel
like an emergency-care system trying to 'help' in a way that becomes
horrifying when nobody is there to override it."*

Two things fall out of the table for free. The **library** is the only place on
the ladder whose last job is directly opposed to the player's — row 46 sends
you to recover records from the one system built to never let records leave.
And the **solar farm** stops being an escalation of difficulty and becomes an
escalation of *category*: the first place whose job was to protect something
from people.

And it names the Warden, which has been called that since the handoff without
anyone saying why:

**The Warden is loss prevention.** It is unkillable because you do not win a
fight with store security, it drops nothing because it carries nothing, it
converges on the brightest position because that is where the activity is, and
it wants you to leave **with nothing** rather than to die — which is row 8,
*only extraction secures loot*, stated as a character. The name was always the
answer.

### Why not the alternatives

- **"They need us for something"** — labour, parts, biology. The genre's
  default, and it is weaker here: a purpose makes them comprehensible, invites
  a *why* chain with no end, and trades the dread of indifference for the
  comfort of a reason. Being wanted is less frightening than being swept up.
- **"They hate us for what we did to the world"** — makes them characters with
  a grievance, which means they can be reasoned with, forgiven, or proven
  right. That is a different game, and a talkier one.
- **"They were reprogrammed / something is controlling them"** — introduces an
  intelligence behind the machines, and then the game owes you that
  intelligence as a boss. The ladder already ends somewhere better.

### What must never be said out loud

Row 43 and `HOW-THE-PLAYER-LEARNS.md` both hold: nobody reads a codex, and
anything needing a paragraph is a thing the game failed to show. **This answer
is the most spoilable thing in the setting.** Stated in a tooltip it is a clever
premise; discovered, it is the moment the game turns over.

So it arrives by arrangement only. A Sweeper working its route through debris
that includes something that was a person, and not reacting. A Stocker
patiently re-shelving. A checkout lane still lit, still waiting. The Warden
arriving at the loading dock, where merchandise leaves.

The player should be able to *reach* this conclusion in hour two and never be
told it. And the Lightkeepers themselves should have it wrong — an order that
has survived generations by telling its children the machines hate them. The
truth is worse, and nobody in the fiction knows it.

**One word is banned, and it is one of mine.** Chris: use *"shoplifter"* **only
as an internal creative shorthand, never as a joke or player-facing
explanation.** It is good shorthand between us and it would be fatal in the
game — it is funny, it is small, and it would convert the best idea in the
setting into a gag in a single line of dialogue. What the player meets is a
dead building's degraded maintenance, safety, stocking and loss-prevention
systems classifying them as an intrusion and an inventory loss. No character,
sign, tooltip, achievement name or store-page blurb gets to be cleverer than
that.
