# Knowledge of place — how a location teaches time and space

Status: **proposed design rule**, 2026-10-31. Chris's correction is
load-bearing: mirrored ceilings belong to some department stores, not all of
them. Every location needs a distinctive way of making knowledge possible,
partial and perishable; it must not receive the same mirror trick in a new
palette.

## The model

Players learn an environment in three compatible forms:

1. **Landmark knowledge** — *I know that object.* A toy sign, escalator well,
   mirrored ceiling island, service clock or bright exit sign establishes a
   reference point.
2. **Route knowledge** — *I know how to get from this thing to that thing.*
   A line of gondolas, an aisle number, a stairwell or a waterline supports a
   repeatable decision at a turn.
3. **Survey knowledge** — *I understand how these areas relate.* A visible
   atrium, floor directory, ceiling grid, exterior view or deliberately
   discovered map lets the crew form a larger mental model.

Wayfinding research consistently finds landmarks most effective when they are
visible on a route and at a decision point; it also distinguishes local
landmarks, which help learn a route, from structural/global cues, which help
form a survey view. The design consequence is simple: a pretty thing at the
end of an aisle is dressing; a distinctive thing at the turn is knowledge.

Time gives that knowledge stakes. A route may be stable; an observation may be
old; a storm flash may reveal a room for one second; a radio report may be
true when spoken and dangerous six minutes later. A game map must never flatten
those into the same kind of fact.

## The contract every new location owes

Before art begins, name five things:

| Question | What must be authored |
| --- | --- |
| **Spatial instrument** | The place-specific feature through which players orient: landmark, path, edge, node or global structure. |
| **Temporal instrument** | The visible/audible rhythm that tells a player something has changed or may be stale: weather, power cycle, water level, machinery cadence, service schedule. |
| **Knowledge gained** | Exactly what it lets a player know: a local turn, vertical relation, route condition, time window, or broad floor relation. |
| **Knowledge withheld** | What it cannot honestly reveal: hidden enemies, off-screen rooms, a current state inferred from an old report. |
| **Counterspace** | Where that instrument fails, forcing another tool, teammate report or deliberate risk. |

This creates a location's *knowledge grammar*. It is not a puzzle gimmick. It
is the reason a player learns one building differently from the next.

## Boswell's example

| Contract part | Boswell's answer |
| --- | --- |
| Spatial instrument | Segmented mirrored ceiling islands over selected main aisles; glass cases, exit signs and the escalator well act as landmarks. |
| Temporal instrument | Weak emergency circuits and storm flashes make some long routes briefly legible; they are orientation cues, never relic-reading light. |
| Knowledge gained | Which department/aisle a keeper is in, where a main aisle reconnects, and a broad relationship to the escalator or service exit. |
| Knowledge withheld | A mirror does not become a real-time enemy radar or a precise floor-plan map. Reflection can be ambiguous, off-angle or absent. |
| Counterspace | Fitting rooms, restrooms, service passages, dense stock and the basement have no helpful ceiling island; this is where the lantern, a discovered map or another keeper's report matters. |

The mirror is therefore a **landmark and partial survey cue**, not a universal
navigation UI. It makes the main retail floor memorable without teaching the
player that looking up is a safe or complete answer.

## The Fieldboard's role

The Fieldboard records **knowledge**, not omniscient state. It separates:

- **Stable discovered structure:** floor outline, a named stairwell, a known
  exit. These remain until the building physically changes.
- **Observed condition:** "East stairwell clear — reported by Mara — 6 min
  ago." This carries a source and age, because it may no longer be true.
- **Immediate sensation:** a threat seen or heard now. This stays in the world
  through telegraphs and does not become a persistent radar marker.

No system may write an observation by itself. A player must see it, inspect
it, trigger a visible response, or communicate it. That preserves the lantern
interpreter rule: the machine makes observable things legible; it never
narrates hidden facts.

## First tests

1. At a main-aisle decision point, can a new player name the landmark they use
   to orient?
2. In the counterspace, can they still recover using their lantern, a map of
   genuinely discovered structure, or a teammate report?
3. Does an old report visibly say who made it and how old it is, rather than
   impersonating a live fact?
4. If the signature feature is removed, does the location lose its particular
   way of teaching space? If not, it is decoration rather than an instrument.

## Research basis

The landmark/route/survey distinction and the importance of visible, salient
landmarks at route decision points are supported by the open literature review
"[Landmarks in wayfinding](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10339-021-01012-x)".
Work on virtual navigation likewise separates landmark, path and location
knowledge ([Muffato et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10397164/)).
For assisted maps, local features improve route knowledge while global
structural features improve survey knowledge, which is why a store should have
both distinctive aisle cues and rare big-picture views
([Löwen, Krukar & Schwering, 2019](https://www.mdpi.com/2220-9964/8/3/149)).
